# Don Quixote - Chapter I

## Before You Read

Before reading, do not flatten Don Quixote into "crazy man reads too much." That is the shallow version. The reason he keeps touching people is that his madness is built out of recognizable needs: to matter, to be brave, to have a story large enough for his life, to turn admiration into action, and to be seen by someone as more than an ordinary aging man in a small place.

Different readers meet him in different places. Young ambitious readers often recognize the hunger to become someone by adopting a model. Older readers feel the ache of a late life suddenly trying to become meaningful. Artists recognize the danger and dignity of imagination. Practical friends and family recognize the pain of watching someone they love become unreachable by facts. That is why the book is not only funny. It is funny because he is wrong; it is moving because the desire underneath the wrongness is human.

Chapter I matters because Cervantes shows this whole human problem before the adventure starts. The man has not yet fought a windmill or met Sancho. He has only rearranged ordinary life into a story. That is already enough to make the reader ask: when does a model give life form, and when does it steal reality?

## Beat-by-Beat Comparison

### Reading Beat 1: Ordinary Life Before the Fantasy

> "An olla of rather more beef than mutton"

Why this matters:

Why this beat matters: Cervantes begins with scale. Food, clothes, household, money, age, and a worn-out horse come before any heroic action. That is the joke and the pathos: the fantasy grows out of a very ordinary life that wants to feel larger than it materially is.

#### Step 1.1: Village, status, and old equipment

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

In a village of La Mancha, the name of which I have no desire to call to mind, there lived not long since one of those gentlemen that keep a lance in the lance-rack, an old buckler, a lean hack, and a greyhound for coursing.

##### Teaching rewrite

In an unnamed village in La Mancha, there lived, not very long ago, a minor country gentleman of the old-fashioned type. He owned the symbols of a gentlemanly life more than the substance of one: a lance on the rack, an old shield, a thin worn-out horse, and a greyhound for hunting.

#### Step 1.2: His money mostly goes to food and appearances

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

An olla of rather more beef than mutton, a salad on most nights, scraps on Saturdays, lentils on Fridays, and a pigeon or so extra on Sundays, made away with three-quarters of his income. The rest of it went in a doublet of fine cloth and velvet breeches and shoes to match for holidays, while on week-days he made a brave figure in his best homespun.

##### Teaching rewrite

Cervantes immediately gives us his food budget because he wants us to know the scale of the man before the fantasy begins. Most of his income went to ordinary meals: a stew with more beef than mutton, salad most nights, leftovers on Saturdays, lentils on Fridays, and a small pigeon dish on Sundays. The rest went to clothing that let him appear respectable on holidays: a fine doublet, velvet breeches, and matching shoes. On weekdays he made do with his best homespun.

#### Step 1.3: The household and the aging gentleman

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

He had in his house a housekeeper past forty, a niece under twenty, and a lad for the field and market-place, who used to saddle the hack as well as handle the bill-hook. The age of this gentleman of ours was bordering on fifty; he was of a hardy habit, spare, gaunt-featured, a very early riser and a great sportsman.

##### Teaching rewrite

His household was small: a housekeeper over forty, a niece under twenty, and a servant boy who handled both farm errands and the horse. The gentleman was around fifty, tough, thin, bony-faced, up early, and fond of hunting.

#### Step 1.4: Even his name is unstable

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

They will have it his surname was Quixada or Quesada (for here there is some difference of opinion among the authors who write on the subject), although from reasonable conjectures it seems plain that he was called Quexana. This, however, is of but little importance to our tale; it will be enough not to stray a hair’s breadth from the truth in the telling of it.

##### Teaching rewrite

Even his real name is uncertain. Some sources call him Quixada, some Quesada, and the narrator thinks Quexana is most likely. Cervantes is already joking like a historian: the story pretends to care about exact truth while admitting that the basic identity of its hero is blurry.

### Reading Beat 2: Books Become His Center of Gravity

> "he sold many an acre of tillageland"

Why this matters:

Why this beat matters: This is not a simple warning against reading. Cervantes is a novelist. The problem is reading that stops answering to life: he sells productive land, neglects ordinary duties, and mistakes foggy language for depth.

#### Step 2.1: Leisure turns into obsession

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

You must know, then, that the above-named gentleman whenever he was at leisure (which was mostly all the year round) gave himself up to reading books of chivalry with such ardour and avidity that he almost entirely neglected the pursuit of his field-sports, and even the management of his property; and to such a pitch did his eagerness and infatuation go that he sold many an acre of tillageland to buy books of chivalry to read, and brought home as many of them as he could get.

##### Teaching rewrite

This gentleman had a great deal of leisure, almost the whole year, and he poured that leisure into books of chivalry. These were adventure romances about wandering knights, giants, enchantments, duels, courtly love, and impossible feats. He read them with such appetite that he neglected hunting and even the management of his property. The obsession became expensive enough that he sold pieces of farmland to buy more romances.

#### Step 2.2: His favorite style is fake-deep fog

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

But of all there were none he liked so well as those of the famous Feliciano de Silva’s composition, for their lucidity of style and complicated conceits were as pearls in his sight, particularly when in his reading he came upon courtships and cartels, where he often found passages like “the reason of the unreason with which my reason is afflicted so weakens my reason that with reason I murmur at your beauty;” or again, “the high heavens, that of your divinity divinely fortify you with the stars, render you deserving of the desert your greatness deserves.”

##### Teaching rewrite

His favorite writer was Feliciano de Silva, not because the prose was clear in any normal sense, but because the gentleman loved its tangled cleverness. Cervantes gives examples of romance language that is almost comic nonsense: phrases where "reason" fights "unreason," or the heavens "divinely" fortify a lady with stars.

#### Step 2.3: He works too hard to understand nonsense

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

Over conceits of this sort the poor gentleman lost his wits, and used to lie awake striving to understand them and worm the meaning out of them; what Aristotle himself could not have made out or extracted had he come to life again for that special purpose.

##### Teaching rewrite

The point is not that Don Quixote understands these sentences deeply. The point is that he treats their fog as profundity. He lies awake trying to squeeze meaning out of them, and the narrator jokes that even Aristotle, raised from the dead for this one job, could not have extracted sense from them.

#### Step 2.4: He starts helping the fiction

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

He was not at all easy about the wounds which Don Belianis gave and took, because it seemed to him that, great as were the surgeons who had cured him, he must have had his face and body covered all over with seams and scars. He commended, however, the author’s way of ending his book with the promise of that interminable adventure, and many a time was he tempted to take up his pen and finish it properly as is there proposed, which no doubt he would have done, and made a successful piece of work of it too, had not greater and more absorbing thoughts prevented him.

##### Teaching rewrite

His reading becomes active, not passive. He worries about continuity errors and physical plausibility inside the romances. When Don Belianis suffers and gives so many wounds, Don Quixote wonders why the knight's face and body are not covered with scars, no matter how good his surgeons were. He admires an author who ends a book by promising an endless adventure, and he is tempted to finish the sequel himself. This matters because Don Quixote is not simply reading for entertainment anymore. He is beginning to collaborate with the fictional world, repair it, extend it, and treat it like a reality that needs his participation.

### Reading Beat 3: His Inner Canon Takes Over

> "the whole fabric of invention and fancy"

Why this matters:

Why this beat matters: The name-drops are not homework. They show that he has built a private operating system out of romance, epic, history, and myth. He ranks heroes the way a modern reader might rank athletes, founders, chess openings, or anime characters.

#### Step 3.1: Village debates become power rankings

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

Many an argument did he have with the curate of his village (a learned man, and a graduate of Siguenza) as to which had been the better knight, Palmerin of England or Amadis of Gaul. Master Nicholas, the village barber, however, used to say that neither of them came up to the Knight of Phœbus, and that if there was any that could compare with him it was Don Galaor, the brother of Amadis of Gaul, because he had a spirit that was equal to every occasion, and was no finikin knight, nor lachrymose like his brother, while in the matter of valour he was not a whit behind him.

##### Teaching rewrite

He argues with the village curate, a formally educated priest, about which fictional or legendary knight is superior: Palmerin of England or Amadis of Gaul. The village barber, Master Nicholas, has his own rankings. He prefers the Knight of Phoebus, and if anyone can stand near him, perhaps Don Galaor, brother of Amadis, because Galaor is steady, flexible, less delicate and tearful than Amadis, and just as brave. These references are not homework for Jerry to memorize. They show the culture Don Quixote has built inside his head: he has a sports-fan or anime-power-scaling relationship to old romance heroes. He knows their traits, debates matchups, ranks temperaments, and treats fictional genealogies as if they were a serious field of knowledge.

#### Step 3.2: Fiction becomes more real than history

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

In short, he became so absorbed in his books that he spent his nights from sunset to sunrise, and his days from dawn to dark, poring over them; and what with little sleep and much reading his brains got so dry that he lost his wits. His fancy grew full of what he used to read about in his books, enchantments, quarrels, battles, challenges, wounds, wooings, loves, agonies, and all sorts of impossible nonsense; and it so possessed his mind that the whole fabric of invention and fancy he read of was true, that to him no history in the world had more reality in it.

##### Teaching rewrite

Then the obsession breaks his judgment. Cervantes gives the famous diagnosis: from little sleep and too much reading, his brain dries up and he loses his wits. His imagination fills with everything his books contain: enchantments, quarrels, battles, challenges, wounds, wooing, love, agony, and every kind of impossible adventure. The crucial sentence is that the whole structure of fiction and fantasy becomes truer to him than any history in the world.

#### Step 3.3: History, myth, and romance collapse together

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

He used to say the Cid Ruy Diaz was a very good knight, but that he was not to be compared with the Knight of the Burning Sword who with one back-stroke cut in half two fierce and monstrous giants. He thought more of Bernardo del Carpio because at Roncesvalles he slew Roland in spite of enchantments, availing himself of the artifice of Hercules when he strangled Antæus the son of Terra in his arms. He approved highly of the giant Morgante, because, although of the giant breed which is always arrogant and ill-conditioned, he alone was affable and well-bred.

##### Teaching rewrite

He compares the historical Spanish hero El Cid with the romance Knight of the Burning Sword, and prefers the impossible hero who can cut two monstrous giants in half with one back-stroke. He admires Bernardo del Carpio because the story says he killed Roland at Roncesvalles, and Cervantes links that victory to the mythic trick of Hercules strangling Antaeus, who drew strength from the earth. He approves of the giant Morgante because, unlike most giants, Morgante is friendly and well-bred.

#### Step 3.4: Old stories now regulate his emotions

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

But above all he admired Reinaldos of Montalban, especially when he saw him sallying forth from his castle and robbing everyone he met, and when beyond the seas he stole that image of Mahomet which, as his history says, was entirely of gold. To have a bout of kicking at that traitor of a Ganelon he would have given his housekeeper, and his niece into the bargain.

##### Teaching rewrite

He especially loves Reinaldos of Montalban when Reinaldos rides out robbing everyone he meets and even steals a golden image of Mahomet overseas. He hates the traitor Ganelon so much that he would give away his housekeeper and his niece for one chance to kick him. This is comic, but also exact: his emotional life is now regulated by a library of inherited stories.

### Reading Beat 4: Fantasy Becomes a Mission

> "right and requisite"

Why this matters:

Why this beat matters: The dangerous shift is that his desired identity starts speaking in the language of duty. He does not think he is choosing a costume; he thinks honor and country require him to become the role he admires.

#### Step 4.1: The fantasy speaks as duty

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

In short, his wits being quite gone, he hit upon the strangest notion that ever madman in this world hit upon, and that was that he fancied it was right and requisite, as well for the support of his own honour as for the service of his country, that he should make a knight-errant of himself, roaming the world over in full armour and on horseback in quest of adventures, and putting in practice himself all that he had read of as being the usual practices of knights-errant; righting every kind of wrong, and exposing himself to peril and danger from which, in the issue, he was to reap eternal renown and fame.

##### Teaching rewrite

Once his judgment is gone, he reaches the strangest decision any madman ever reached: he decides that both his honor and his country's welfare require him to become a knight-errant. A knight-errant is a wandering knight who rides through the world looking for adventures, righting wrongs, protecting the weak, defeating monsters, and winning fame.

#### Step 4.2: Pleasure becomes action

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

Already the poor man saw himself crowned by the might of his arm Emperor of Trebizond at least; and so, led away by the intense enjoyment he found in these pleasant fancies, he set himself forthwith to put his scheme into execution.

##### Teaching rewrite

Don Quixote does not merely want to admire that role. He wants to perform it in the present day, in real armor, on a real horse. He imagines himself exposing his body to danger and emerging with eternal renown. Already he sees himself, at minimum, crowned Emperor of Trebizond, which is the kind of far-off romance prize that sounds grander than his actual village life. The fantasy gives him pleasure, and because it gives him pleasure, he moves from imagining to executing.

### Reading Beat 5: Reality Fails the Helmet Test

> "what had taken him a week to do"

Why this matters:

Why this beat matters: This is the cleanest life-pattern in the chapter. Reality gives him a valid test result: the pasteboard helmet fails. Instead of updating the fantasy, he weakens the test, reinforces the helmet, and refuses to test again.

Jerry handle: this is the part to remember outside the novel. When a project, identity, relationship idea, career plan, or self-image fails a real test, do you update the idea, or do you quietly stop testing it? Don Quixote does not lie in a simple way. He arranges reality so the role can keep going.

#### Step 5.1: Old armor gets turned into a prop

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

The first thing he did was to clean up some armour that had belonged to his great-grandfather, and had been for ages lying forgotten in a corner eaten with rust and covered with mildew. He scoured and polished it as best he could, but he perceived one great defect in it, that it had no closed helmet, nothing but a simple morion. This deficiency, however, his ingenuity supplied, for he contrived a kind of half-helmet of pasteboard which, fitted on to the morion, looked like a whole one.

##### Teaching rewrite

His first practical task is to restore armor that had belonged to his great-grandfather. It has been lying in a corner for ages, rusty and mildewed. He cleans and polishes it as well as he can. The problem is that the helmet is incomplete: he has only an open morion, not a closed knightly helmet. So he improvises with pasteboard and turns the morion into something that looks like a full helmet.

#### Step 5.2: The failed test is quietly weakened

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

It is true that, in order to see if it was strong and fit to stand a cut, he drew his sword and gave it a couple of slashes, the first of which undid in an instant what had taken him a week to do. The ease with which he had knocked it to pieces disconcerted him somewhat, and to guard against that danger he set to work again, fixing bars of iron on the inside until he was satisfied with its strength; and then, not caring to try any more experiments with it, he passed it and adopted it as a helmet of the most perfect construction.

##### Teaching rewrite

Then he tests it. One sword blow destroys in a moment what took him a week to build. For a second reality gives clear feedback: the helmet is fake. But instead of accepting the result, he reinforces it with iron bars, decides it is strong enough, and refuses to test it again. This is the chapter in miniature. The world disproves the fantasy; he modifies the evidence procedure, not the fantasy.

### Reading Beat 6: Naming the Horse and Himself

> "taking a new character, he should take a new name"

Why this matters:

Why this beat matters: The horse is still a bad horse and the man is still from La Mancha, but names let ordinary things enter the romance system. Rocinante and Don Quixote of La Mancha are not just labels; they are attempts to make a new identity feel real.

#### Step 6.1: A poor horse becomes heroic material

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

He next proceeded to inspect his hack, which, with more quartos than a real and more blemishes than the steed of Gonela, that “tantum pellis et ossa fuit,” surpassed in his eyes the Bucephalus of Alexander or the Babieca of the Cid. Four days were spent in thinking what name to give him,

##### Teaching rewrite

Next he inspects his horse. The animal is a wreck, more blemished than valuable, closer to skin and bones than heroic steed.

#### Step 6.2: The name must explain before and after

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

because (as he said to himself) it was not right that a horse belonging to a knight so famous, and one with such merits of his own, should be without some distinctive name, and he strove to adapt it so as to indicate what he had been before belonging to a knight-errant, and what he then was; for it was only reasonable that, his master taking a new character, he should take a new name, and that it should be a distinguished and full-sounding one, befitting the new order and calling he was about to follow.

##### Teaching rewrite

But Don Quixote now reads the horse through the same heroic script. To his eyes, this poor hack surpasses Alexander's Bucephalus and the Cid's Babieca. He spends four days inventing a name, because a knight's horse cannot remain an ordinary farm animal with an ordinary identity. The name must express both what the horse used to be and what it has now become.

#### Step 6.3: Rocinante completes the transformation

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

And so, after having composed, struck out, rejected, added to, unmade, and remade a multitude of names out of his memory and fancy, he decided upon calling him Rocinante, a name, to his thinking, lofty, sonorous, and significant of his condition as a hack before he became what he now was, the first and foremost of all the hacks in the world.

##### Teaching rewrite

After composing, rejecting, adding, erasing, and remaking many possibilities, he chooses Rocinante. The narrator explains the joke inside the name: it suggests a hack before, but also the foremost hack now, the first among all hacks. Don Quixote turns a bad horse into a literary object by naming it.

#### Step 6.4: The man names himself into the role

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

Having got a name for his horse so much to his taste, he was anxious to get one for himself, and he was eight days more pondering over this point, till at last he made up his mind to call himself “Don Quixote,”

##### Teaching rewrite

Once the horse has a heroic name, the man needs one too. He thinks for eight more days and chooses Don Quixote.

#### Step 6.5: La Mancha is pulled into the myth

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

whence, as has been already said, the authors of this veracious history have inferred that his name must have been beyond a doubt Quixada, and not Quesada as others would have it. Recollecting, however, that the valiant Amadis was not content to call himself curtly Amadis and nothing more, but added the name of his kingdom and country to make it famous, and called himself Amadis of Gaul, he, like a good knight, resolved to add on the name of his, and to style himself Don Quixote of La Mancha, whereby, he considered, he described accurately his origin and country, and did honour to it in taking his surname from it.

##### Teaching rewrite

The narrator then loops back to the earlier uncertainty over his real surname, as if this chosen name proves that his old name must have been Quixada, not Quesada. Don Quixote remembers that Amadis did not merely call himself Amadis; he became Amadis of Gaul, tying himself to a kingdom and making that place famous. So this gentleman adds his region too and becomes Don Quixote of La Mancha. The joke is that La Mancha is ordinary and dry, not the glamorous kingdom of romance. But the move is also serious: identity now has three parts, role, self, and place. He is not just changing his name. He is making his village life enter a literary mythology.

### Reading Beat 7: Inventing Dulcinea

> "she never knew it nor gave a thought to the matter"

Why this matters:

Why this beat matters: Dulcinea shows the same pattern applied to love. Aldonza Lorenzo is a real farm girl who apparently never participated in his feeling; Don Quixote turns her into a required figure in his story. The role comes first, the relationship second or not at all.

End spoiler that changes the beginning: at the end of the novel he recovers sanity, renounces chivalric books, returns to the name Alonso Quixano, and dies. So Chapter I is not merely "funny old guy chooses a costume." It is the start of a whole life being organized by names and roles, then painfully disorganized when the names stop working.

#### Step 7.1: Love is treated as a required slot

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

So then, his armour being furbished, his morion turned into a helmet, his hack christened, and he himself confirmed, he came to the conclusion that nothing more was needed now but to look out for a lady to be in love with; for a knight-errant without love was like a tree without leaves or fruit, or a body without a soul.

##### Teaching rewrite

With armor cleaned, helmet improvised, horse named, and self renamed, he sees only one missing requirement: a lady to love. In the romance code, a knight without love is like a tree without leaves or fruit, or a body without a soul.

#### Step 7.2: He imagines the giant before the woman

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

As he said to himself, “If, for my sins, or by my good fortune, I come across some giant hereabouts, a common occurrence with knights-errant, and overthrow him in one onslaught, or cleave him asunder to the waist, or, in short, vanquish and subdue him, will it not be well to have someone I may send him to as a present, that he may come in and fall on his knees before my sweet lady,

##### Teaching rewrite

He imagines the future scene in detail. If he meets a giant, which he assumes is a normal thing for knights, he will defeat him, cut him down, or subdue him, and then send the conquered giant to kneel before his lady.

#### Step 7.3: The speech is already scripted

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

and in a humble, submissive voice say, ‘I am the giant Caraculiambro, lord of the island of Malindrania, vanquished in single combat by the never sufficiently extolled knight Don Quixote of La Mancha, who has commanded me to present myself before your Grace, that your Highness dispose of me at your pleasure’?” Oh, how our good gentleman enjoyed the delivery of this speech, especially when he had thought of someone to call his Lady!

##### Teaching rewrite

He even invents the speech the giant will deliver: "I am the giant Caraculiambro, lord of the island of Malindrania, defeated by the never-sufficiently-praised Don Quixote of La Mancha, who sends me to your Grace so your Highness may dispose of me." This is not love arising from relationship. It is a required slot in a story template.

#### Step 7.4: Aldonza is real, but not involved

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

There was, so the story goes, in a village near his own a very good-looking farm-girl with whom he had been at one time in love, though, so far as is known, she never knew it nor gave a thought to the matter.

##### Teaching rewrite

At last he chooses the woman who will fill that slot. Near his village there is a good-looking farm girl named Aldonza Lorenzo. He had once been in love with her, although she apparently never knew it and never cared.

#### Step 7.5: Dulcinea completes the story-world

##### Near-original reading copy (source-close; wording preserved)

Her name was Aldonza Lorenzo, and upon her he thought fit to confer the title of Lady of his Thoughts; and after some search for a name which should not be out of harmony with her own, and should suggest and indicate that of a princess and great lady, he decided upon calling her Dulcinea del Toboso—she being of El Toboso—a name, to his mind, musical, uncommon, and significant, like all those he had already bestowed upon himself and the things belonging to him.

##### Teaching rewrite

He now elevates her into the Lady of his Thoughts. Since Aldonza Lorenzo sounds too ordinary for the role, he searches for a name that will harmonize with hers while sounding like a princess or great lady. Because she is from El Toboso, he calls her Dulcinea del Toboso. The name sounds sweet, unusual, musical, and important to him, just like the names he has already given his horse, himself, and everything around him. The chapter ends with the machinery complete: not a real knight, not a real helmet, not a noble horse, not a mutual beloved, but a whole interpreted world in which each ordinary thing has been renamed until it can play a heroic part.

## Original Source

In a village of La Mancha, the name of which I have no desire to call to mind, there lived not long since one of those gentlemen that keep a lance in the lance-rack, an old buckler, a lean hack, and a greyhound for coursing. An olla of rather more beef than mutton, a salad on most nights, scraps on Saturdays, lentils on Fridays, and a pigeon or so extra on Sundays, made away with three-quarters of his income. The rest of it went in a doublet of fine cloth and velvet breeches and shoes to match for holidays, while on week-days he made a brave figure in his best homespun. He had in his house a housekeeper past forty, a niece under twenty, and a lad for the field and market-place, who used to saddle the hack as well as handle the bill-hook. The age of this gentleman of ours was bordering on fifty; he was of a hardy habit, spare, gaunt-featured, a very early riser and a great sportsman. They will have it his surname was Quixada or Quesada (for here there is some difference of opinion among the authors who write on the subject), although from reasonable conjectures it seems plain that he was called Quexana. This, however, is of but little importance to our tale; it will be enough not to stray a hair’s breadth from the truth in the telling of it.

You must know, then, that the above-named gentleman whenever he was at leisure (which was mostly all the year round) gave himself up to reading books of chivalry with such ardour and avidity that he almost entirely neglected the pursuit of his field-sports, and even the management of his property; and to such a pitch did his eagerness and infatuation go that he sold many an acre of tillageland to buy books of chivalry to read, and brought home as many of them as he could get. But of all there were none he liked so well as those of the famous Feliciano de Silva’s composition, for their lucidity of style and complicated conceits were as pearls in his sight, particularly when in his reading he came upon courtships and cartels, where he often found passages like “the reason of the unreason with which my reason is afflicted so weakens my reason that with reason I murmur at your beauty;” or again, “the high heavens, that of your divinity divinely fortify you with the stars, render you deserving of the desert your greatness deserves.” Over conceits of this sort the poor gentleman lost his wits, and used to lie awake striving to understand them and worm the meaning out of them; what Aristotle himself could not have made out or extracted had he come to life again for that special purpose. He was not at all easy about the wounds which Don Belianis gave and took, because it seemed to him that, great as were the surgeons who had cured him, he must have had his face and body covered all over with seams and scars. He commended, however, the author’s way of ending his book with the promise of that interminable adventure, and many a time was he tempted to take up his pen and finish it properly as is there proposed, which no doubt he would have done, and made a successful piece of work of it too, had not greater and more absorbing thoughts prevented him.

Many an argument did he have with the curate of his village (a learned man, and a graduate of Siguenza) as to which had been the better knight, Palmerin of England or Amadis of Gaul. Master Nicholas, the village barber, however, used to say that neither of them came up to the Knight of Phœbus, and that if there was any that could compare with him it was Don Galaor, the brother of Amadis of Gaul, because he had a spirit that was equal to every occasion, and was no finikin knight, nor lachrymose like his brother, while in the matter of valour he was not a whit behind him. In short, he became so absorbed in his books that he spent his nights from sunset to sunrise, and his days from dawn to dark, poring over them; and what with little sleep and much reading his brains got so dry that he lost his wits. His fancy grew full of what he used to read about in his books, enchantments, quarrels, battles, challenges, wounds, wooings, loves, agonies, and all sorts of impossible nonsense; and it so possessed his mind that the whole fabric of invention and fancy he read of was true, that to him no history in the world had more reality in it. He used to say the Cid Ruy Diaz was a very good knight, but that he was not to be compared with the Knight of the Burning Sword who with one back-stroke cut in half two fierce and monstrous giants. He thought more of Bernardo del Carpio because at Roncesvalles he slew Roland in spite of enchantments, availing himself of the artifice of Hercules when he strangled Antæus the son of Terra in his arms. He approved highly of the giant Morgante, because, although of the giant breed which is always arrogant and ill-conditioned, he alone was affable and well-bred. But above all he admired Reinaldos of Montalban, especially when he saw him sallying forth from his castle and robbing everyone he met, and when beyond the seas he stole that image of Mahomet which, as his history says, was entirely of gold. To have a bout of kicking at that traitor of a Ganelon he would have given his housekeeper, and his niece into the bargain.

In short, his wits being quite gone, he hit upon the strangest notion that ever madman in this world hit upon, and that was that he fancied it was right and requisite, as well for the support of his own honour as for the service of his country, that he should make a knight-errant of himself, roaming the world over in full armour and on horseback in quest of adventures, and putting in practice himself all that he had read of as being the usual practices of knights-errant; righting every kind of wrong, and exposing himself to peril and danger from which, in the issue, he was to reap eternal renown and fame. Already the poor man saw himself crowned by the might of his arm Emperor of Trebizond at least; and so, led away by the intense enjoyment he found in these pleasant fancies, he set himself forthwith to put his scheme into execution.

The first thing he did was to clean up some armour that had belonged to his great-grandfather, and had been for ages lying forgotten in a corner eaten with rust and covered with mildew. He scoured and polished it as best he could, but he perceived one great defect in it, that it had no closed helmet, nothing but a simple morion. This deficiency, however, his ingenuity supplied, for he contrived a kind of half-helmet of pasteboard which, fitted on to the morion, looked like a whole one. It is true that, in order to see if it was strong and fit to stand a cut, he drew his sword and gave it a couple of slashes, the first of which undid in an instant what had taken him a week to do. The ease with which he had knocked it to pieces disconcerted him somewhat, and to guard against that danger he set to work again, fixing bars of iron on the inside until he was satisfied with its strength; and then, not caring to try any more experiments with it, he passed it and adopted it as a helmet of the most perfect construction.

He next proceeded to inspect his hack, which, with more quartos than a real and more blemishes than the steed of Gonela, that “tantum pellis et ossa fuit,” surpassed in his eyes the Bucephalus of Alexander or the Babieca of the Cid. Four days were spent in thinking what name to give him, because (as he said to himself) it was not right that a horse belonging to a knight so famous, and one with such merits of his own, should be without some distinctive name, and he strove to adapt it so as to indicate what he had been before belonging to a knight-errant, and what he then was; for it was only reasonable that, his master taking a new character, he should take a new name, and that it should be a distinguished and full-sounding one, befitting the new order and calling he was about to follow. And so, after having composed, struck out, rejected, added to, unmade, and remade a multitude of names out of his memory and fancy, he decided upon calling him Rocinante, a name, to his thinking, lofty, sonorous, and significant of his condition as a hack before he became what he now was, the first and foremost of all the hacks in the world.

Having got a name for his horse so much to his taste, he was anxious to get one for himself, and he was eight days more pondering over this point, till at last he made up his mind to call himself “Don Quixote,” whence, as has been already said, the authors of this veracious history have inferred that his name must have been beyond a doubt Quixada, and not Quesada as others would have it. Recollecting, however, that the valiant Amadis was not content to call himself curtly Amadis and nothing more, but added the name of his kingdom and country to make it famous, and called himself Amadis of Gaul, he, like a good knight, resolved to add on the name of his, and to style himself Don Quixote of La Mancha, whereby, he considered, he described accurately his origin and country, and did honour to it in taking his surname from it.

So then, his armour being furbished, his morion turned into a helmet, his hack christened, and he himself confirmed, he came to the conclusion that nothing more was needed now but to look out for a lady to be in love with; for a knight-errant without love was like a tree without leaves or fruit, or a body without a soul. As he said to himself, “If, for my sins, or by my good fortune, I come across some giant hereabouts, a common occurrence with knights-errant, and overthrow him in one onslaught, or cleave him asunder to the waist, or, in short, vanquish and subdue him, will it not be well to have someone I may send him to as a present, that he may come in and fall on his knees before my sweet lady, and in a humble, submissive voice say, ‘I am the giant Caraculiambro, lord of the island of Malindrania, vanquished in single combat by the never sufficiently extolled knight Don Quixote of La Mancha, who has commanded me to present myself before your Grace, that your Highness dispose of me at your pleasure’?” Oh, how our good gentleman enjoyed the delivery of this speech, especially when he had thought of someone to call his Lady! There was, so the story goes, in a village near his own a very good-looking farm-girl with whom he had been at one time in love, though, so far as is known, she never knew it nor gave a thought to the matter. Her name was Aldonza Lorenzo, and upon her he thought fit to confer the title of Lady of his Thoughts; and after some search for a name which should not be out of harmony with her own, and should suggest and indicate that of a princess and great lady, he decided upon calling her Dulcinea del Toboso—she being of El Toboso—a name, to his mind, musical, uncommon, and significant, like all those he had already bestowed upon himself and the things belonging to him.
