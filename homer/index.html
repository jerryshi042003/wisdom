The finite path — four stops, then you're done

This is a ladder, not a rabbit hole. Everything else on this page is context for these four.

  1. The Odyssey — Emily Wilson translation (2017) A soldier takes ten years to come home, and home has to decide if it still knows him. Read Jerry's Norton edition continuously; use the internal 24-book companion only for friction, translation choices, and output.
  2. The Iliad — Wilson (2023) Fifty-one days of the war, ten years before the Odyssey. Achilles' rage, Hector's doom. Read it second so the world Odysseus returns from acquires human cost rather than becoming prerequisite homework.
  3. Virgil, Aeneid — Robert Fagles translation Rome rewrites both poems from the Trojan losers' side: wandering becomes national destiny and a new violent ending argues with Homer's imposed peace.
  4. Aeschylus, Agamemnon (one evening) The homecoming that goes wrong — the dark mirror the Odyssey itself keeps pointing at. Then sample the Hymn to Demeter only if the gods and ritual world pull you further.

Driving option: use the Ian McKellen reading of Fagles as a performance comparison, not as a replacement for the Wilson book you are annotating.

The author question

Was Homer one person? Probably not — and the truth is better

There is zero biographical evidence: no contemporary source, no dates, no grave. Tradition made him a blind bard from Chios, ~8th century BC.

The real story: in the 1920s–30s Milman Parry proved the poems are oral-formulaic composition. The repeated tags — "swift-footed Achilles," "wine-dark sea," "rosy-fingered dawn" — are metrical building blocks that let illiterate bards improvise hours of verse live. Parry then recorded Balkan bards doing exactly this with poems of Homeric length. The epics crystallize centuries of oral tradition: the Trojan War material is ~400 years older than the poems themselves.

Best current view: one or two master poets consolidated the tradition into these exact poems around 750–700 BC, right when the Greek alphabet arrived. Many scholars think the Iliad and Odyssey are by different people — the Odyssey reads a generation later, with different vocabulary and a different moral world. "Homer" is best understood as the name the tradition gave itself.

The poems describe a Bronze Age world (~1200 BC) remembered across four dark centuries. Troy is real — the mound at Hisarlik, Turkey, excavated from the 1870s — but no one can prove the war.

Epic one

The Iliad — rage, honor, and one man's death

What it actually covers: not the whole war — about 51 days of year nine. No Trojan Horse. No fall of Troy. No Achilles' heel. It opens with Achilles' rage at his own commander and closes with a funeral. Everything is compressed into one question: what is a short glorious life worth?

"Sing, goddess, the rage of Peleus' son Achilles…" — the first word of Western literature is rage (mēnin).
Book-by-book (24 books, one line each)
1Agamemnon confiscates Achilles' war-prize Briseis; Achilles quits the war and asks Zeus to make the Greeks lose.
2Zeus sends a lying dream; catalogue of the thousand ships — the whole coalition on parade.
3Paris vs. Menelaus in single combat for Helen; Aphrodite cheats Paris out of death.
4The truce is broken by an arrow; the war restarts for good.
5Diomedes goes berserk and wounds two literal gods.
6The famous still point: Hector goes home, holds his infant son, who cries at his father's helmet. He takes it off.
7Hector duels Ajax to a draw; both sides bury their dead.
8Zeus tilts the battle; Greeks driven back to their ships.
9The embassy: Agamemnon offers Achilles a fortune to return. Achilles says no — the poem's moral core: no treasure equals a life.
10Night raid; Odysseus and Diomedes butcher sleeping allies. (Many scholars think this book is a later insert.)
11Agamemnon's best day, then wounded; the Greek captains fall one by one.
12Hector smashes through the Greek wall.
13Poseidon secretly rallies the Greeks at the ships.
14Hera seduces Zeus to distract him — the gods play soap opera while men die.
15Zeus wakes furious; Hector reaches the ships with fire.
16The hinge: Patroclus borrows Achilles' armor, drives the Trojans back, and is killed by Hector.
17Battle over Patroclus' body; even Achilles' immortal horses weep.
18Achilles' scream. His mother brings new armor; on the shield the god forges the entire world — weddings, harvests, cities at peace and war.
19Achilles and Agamemnon reconcile; the quarrel no longer matters and both know it.
20The gods themselves join the battle.
21Achilles chokes the river with corpses; the river god rises to drown him.
22Hector, alone outside the walls, runs three laps of Troy — then stands, and dies. Achilles drags the body.
23Funeral games for Patroclus; grief converted into sport.
24The ending nothing prepares you for: old Priam sneaks into Achilles' tent at night, kisses the hands that killed his son, and asks for the body. They weep together. Hector is buried. End.
Epic two

The Odyssey — the ten-year commute

What it actually covers: the war has been over ten years. Odysseus is the last man not home. Structure is modern: it starts with the son, jumps to Odysseus in year seven of captivity, then has him narrate the famous monsters as a flashback at a dinner party (books 9–12 — the part every adaptation strip-mines). The whole second half is the homecoming itself: disguise, testing, revenge.

The opening calls Odysseus polytropos: many-turning, versatile, wandering, tactically intelligent. Every English version must choose which part becomes foreground.
Book-by-book (24 books, one line each)
1Ithaca overrun by Penelope's suitors; Athena nudges the son, Telemachus, to grow up.
2Telemachus calls an assembly, is laughed at, and sails off to find news of his father.
3Old king Nestor tells war stories; first warning-echo of Agamemnon's murder.
4Menelaus and Helen, home and uneasy; the suitors plot to kill Telemachus.
5Odysseus at last — weeping on Calypso's beach, offered immortality, choosing home instead. Raft, storm, shipwreck.
6Washed up naked; princess Nausicaa finds him. (Yes — Miyazaki's Nausicaä is named after her.)
7The Phaeacian palace takes him in, name unknown.
8A bard sings of Troy; Odysseus weeps at his own story — then agrees to tell it.
9The flashback begins: Lotus-eaters, then the Cyclops — the trick name "Nobody," the blinding, and the fatal boast that curses the trip.
10The bag of winds opened in sight of home; Circe turns the crew to pigs.
11The underworld: his mother's ghost, blind Tiresias' prophecy, and Achilles' retraction — "I'd rather be a living slave than king of all the dead."
12Sirens (tied to the mast), Scylla eats six men, the crew eats the Sun-god's cattle and is annihilated. Flashback ends.
13The Phaeacians ferry him home asleep; he wakes on Ithaca and doesn't recognize it. Athena disguises him as a beggar.
14The loyal swineherd Eumaeus feeds a "stranger" — hospitality as the poem's moral test.
15Telemachus dodges the ambush and comes home.
16Father and son reunited in a hut; the revenge is planned.
17The beggar enters his own palace; only his old dog Argos knows him — wags once, and dies.
18Forced to box another beggar for the suitors' entertainment in his own hall.
19The long fireside interview with Penelope, who may suspect; the old nurse recognizes his scar.
20Omens pile up; the suitors laugh with blood on their faces (the poem's eeriest image).
21Penelope's contest: string Odysseus' bow. Only the beggar can. He strings it, and the door is barred.
22The hall becomes a killing floor — every suitor dies.
23Penelope's own test — "move the bed" — which only the real Odysseus knows is impossible. Reunion, twenty years late.
24Peace is imposed between Odysseus and the suitors' families; the cycle of revenge is stopped by a goddess. End.
A connected side corridor

Orpheus, Black Orpheus, and jazz are not another Odyssey

They belong to different branches. The useful connection is not a shared plot or author; it is how each work transforms danger, death, memory, and song.

Odyssey = return

A living war survivor tries to become recognizable at home again.

↕ Sirens · underworld · voice · memory

Orpheus = retrieve

A musician enters death for one beloved person and loses her when doubt demands visual proof.

↓ myth relocated into another people, city, and medium

Black Orpheus = relocate

The 1956 Rio play and 1959 film place Orpheus in Black Brazilian performance and Carnival—liberating the classical image while raising the question of who controls the frame.

↓ film melody becomes a shared standard

Jazz = improvise

Luiz Bonfá's Manhã de Carnaval, often titled “Black Orpheus” in jazz, carries emotional memory rather than retelling the myth. Different players inherit one melody and find a route back together.

The two direct ancient bridges

Sirens: Odysseus uses wax, rope, hierarchy, and a binding rule. In Apollonius's Argonautica, Orpheus answers the Sirens with a stronger communal song. Ask whether danger needs restraint or a better competing form.

The dead: Odysseus goes down mainly to obtain information that can get him home. Orpheus goes down to recover Eurydice. One returns with knowledge; the other discovers that art can move death but cannot abolish mortality or doubt.

Useful anchors after the first uninterrupted watch/listen
  • Orpheus animation, 0:54: music opens the underworld where force cannot; 2:17–2:35: silence becomes doubt and the backward look destroys the return.
  • Black Orpheus, 107-minute cut, 57:30–59:00: guitar, children, and sunrise make art a social belief; 89:00–97:00: bureaucracy and spiritual ceremony remake the underworld and backward look.
  • Emmet Cohen ensemble, 0:58 / 4:50 / 7:28 / 8:50: clarinet, trumpet, piano, and bass inherit the tune; 11:30: the group returns toward the shared melody.

Carry four verbs, not four author names: return · retrieve · relocate · improvise. The full timestamp map also lives inside the Odyssey companion, next to Books 11–12 where it becomes useful.

The lost six

The Epic Cycle — where the famous stuff actually was

Around the two surviving epics stood six more connected Trojan epics. All are lost — we have ancient plot summaries (Proclus) and fragments. This is why the Trojan Horse and Achilles' death are not in the Iliad: they were in these. Nolan's film stitched together material Homer never wrote.

  • Cypria — the war's origins: the golden apple, the Judgment of Paris, Helen taken, Iphigenia sacrificed for wind.
  • Aethiopis — Achilles kills the Amazon queen Penthesilea (and mourns her), then dies from Paris' arrow.
  • Little Iliad — the armor dispute drives Ajax mad; the Horse is built.
  • Sack of Troy (Iliou Persis) — the Horse enters; Troy burns; the crimes committed that night curse the victors' journeys home.
  • Returns (Nostoi) — every hero's voyage home, including Agamemnon's murder in his bathtub.
  • Telegony — antiquity's own bad fan-fiction: Odysseus' son by Circe kills him by accident, then marries Penelope. The ancients kept it; you can skip it.

Read Proclus's ancient summaries before buying a fragment collection; the six epics do not survive as complete books.

The rest of "Homer"

Hymns, Hesiod, and the fan-fiction that became literature

Homeric Hymns

33 hymns to the gods in Homer's meter — attributed to him in antiquity, actually various later poets. Only two are essential: Demeter (Persephone, the seasons, grief) and Hermes (a newborn god steals Apollo's cattle and invents the lyre — genuinely funny).

Hesiod (~700 BC) — Homer's only peer

Theogony: the gods' complete family tree, from Chaos to Zeus — the operating manual for every myth you half-know. Works and Days: a grumpy farmer lectures his deadbeat brother about work, justice, and when to plant — the anti-heroic voice of the same world. Both are short; choose a modern annotated edition only if this branch earns the time.

Tragedy (400s BC)

Athens' playwrights treated the epics as a shared universe. The essential sequel: Aeschylus' Agamemnon — the commander comes home to a wife who has waited ten years with a plan. It is the Odyssey inverted, and the Odyssey itself keeps citing this exact story as Odysseus' nightmare scenario. Then, optionally: Euripides' Trojan Women (the sack from the victims' side) and Cyclops (the Polyphemus episode as comedy — our only complete satyr play).

Virgil, Aeneid (~19 BC)

Rome's answer, written 700 years later in Latin: a minor Trojan survivor escapes the burning city carrying his father, wanders (Odyssey half), fights for Italy (Iliad half), and founds the line that becomes Rome. The first great literary reply — the losers get the last word.

Survival

How a pre-literate poem outlived every empire

  • ~750–700 BC — composed orally, right as the Greek alphabet arrives.
  • 500s BC — Athens standardizes the text; the Panathenaea festival requires rhapsodes to perform Homer in order.
  • 300–150 BC — the Library of Alexandria edits it (Zenodotus → Aristarchus): suspect lines flagged, the 24-book divisions imposed. Our book numbers are theirs.
  • Papyri from Egypt — show "wild" variant texts before Alexandria, stable text after. The editing worked.
  • 900s AD — oldest complete Iliad manuscript (Venetus A, Byzantine, now in Venice) — ~1,700 years after composition, remarkably faithful.
  • 1488 — first printed edition, Florence. 1616 — Chapman's English (the one that floored Keats). Then Pope, and the 20th-century line: Lattimore → Fitzgerald → Fagles → Wilson.
Translations

Which English Homer — choose by purpose, not winner

The opening adjective polytropos spans turning, wandering, versatility, and tactical intelligence. That is the method: compare one loaded decision, then return to the same continuous edition.

TranslatorUseWhat to watch
Wilson (2017/2023)Continuous first readFast blank verse with visible attention to social rank, enslaved status, gendered judgment, and the pressure of one English line per Greek line.
Fagles (1990/96)Performance / audiobookRhetorical momentum and a larger spoken register. Compare after a scene, not paragraph by paragraph during the first read.
Lattimore (1951/65)Disputed-word referenceCloser syntactic pressure and repeated formulas. Use it when one noun or verb matters, not as obligatory parallel reading.

Translation method: ask what formal constraint the translator chose, which social category an English word creates, what ambiguity disappears, and whether that decision changes blame. No full modern translation is hosted here.
