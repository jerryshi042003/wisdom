## What Actually Matters in Ivan Ilyich

This guide exists because the novella can look simple while the social, French, medical, religious, and Russian-name signals do the real work. The default reader keeps the full edition and adds explanation where Jerry is likely to miss how a phrase changes the reading.

### 1. Read it as a full novella, not a moral summary

> Ivan Ilych's life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible.

The reader keeps the whole Maude translation. Tolstoy's force comes from repetition, social detail, and the slow tightening of Ivan's private terror. Do not skip to the idea that he learns death is real; the book is about how a whole respectable life trains him not to see it.

### 2. The social language is part of the disease

> comme il faut

French phrases and words like propriety, decorum, correctitude, and comme il faut are not decorative. They name Ivan's operating system: life judged by what refined society approves. The guided text glosses these moments because they are the key to why his life feels successful and empty at the same time.

### 3. Russian names carry relationship distance

> Praskovya Fedorovna

The text uses formal Russian names, family names, nicknames, and a French household name for Ivan. The reader does not need to master Russian naming rules first; the guide identifies the recurring roles: Praskovya is the wife, Gerasim is the servant who can face death honestly, Volodya/Vasya is the son, Lisa is the daughter, and Schwartz is the social friend who keeps the card-game world intact.

### 4. The medical plot is deliberately evasive

> floating kidney, chronic catarrh, or appendicitis

The disease is never cleanly named. That is the point. Doctors convert death into technical language about kidneys, appendix, absorption, and organs. Ivan clings to that language because it lets him think he has a case to manage instead of a life to judge.

### 5. Gerasim is not a sentimental side character

> We shall all come to it some day.

Gerasim matters because he does not protect himself with the social lie. He can hold Ivan's legs, admit death, and pity him without turning the room into performance. He is the opposite of Ivan's entire official/social world.

### 6. The ending is religious without becoming a slogan

> Instead of death there was light.

Tolstoy's ending is not merely 'be nicer before you die.' Ivan's breakthrough is pity: he sees the suffering he is causing and stops defending the false life. The reader should notice that the release comes when attention turns outward, not when Ivan wins an argument about illness.

## Chapter I: The Funeral and the Avoided Warning

### Before you read

Before reading: the opening is after Ivan's death. Watch how quickly his colleagues turn death into promotions, inconvenience, etiquette, and card games. Schwartz is the polished friend who represents social normality continuing as if nothing ultimate has happened.

Name handles: Praskovya Fedorovna is Ivan's widow; Peter Ivanovich is the colleague whose viewpoint frames the funeral; Gerasim is the servant who will later become the honest counterweight to everyone else.

### Jerry Edition (direct rewrite across the full novella; preserves source order and important quotes)

During a break in the Melvinski trial, several judges and the public prosecutor gathered in Ivan Egorovich Shebek's private room at the Law Courts. They were talking about the famous Krasovski case. Fedor Vasilievich argued strongly that it did not belong under their court's authority. Ivan Egorovich argued the opposite. Peter Ivanovich had not joined the argument from the beginning, so he stayed out of it and instead looked through the newly delivered Gazette.

"Gentlemen," he said, "Ivan Ilych has died!"

"You don't say so!"

"Here, read it yourself," Peter Ivanovich said, handing Fedor Vasilievich the still-damp newspaper. In a black-bordered notice it said: "Praskovya Fedorovna Golovina, with profound sorrow, informs relatives and friends of the death of her beloved husband Ivan Ilych Golovin, Member of the Court of Justice, which occurred on February 4 of this year, 1882. The funeral will take place on Friday at one o'clock in the afternoon."

Ivan Ilych had been their colleague, and they had all liked him. He had been sick for several weeks with an illness people said could not be cured. His position had been held open, but everyone had already been guessing what would happen if he died: Alexeev might get Ivan's post, and either Vinnikov or Shtabel might get Alexeev's. So when the men in that private room heard Ivan Ilych was dead, each man's first thought was not "death has come close to us," but "what promotions or transfers might this open for me or my acquaintances?"

"I shall certainly get Shtabel's place, or Vinnikov's," thought Fedor Vasilievich. "That was promised to me long ago, and it would mean eight hundred extra rubles a year, besides the allowance."

"Now I must apply to have my brother-in-law transferred from Kaluga," thought Peter Ivanovich. "My wife will be glad, and then she won't be able to say I never do anything for her family."

"I thought he would never get out of bed again," Peter Ivanovich said aloud. "It is very sad."

"But what was actually wrong with him?"

"The doctors could not say. Or rather, they could say, but each one said something different. When I last saw him, I thought he was getting better."

"And I have not seen him since the holidays. I always meant to go."

"Did he have any property?"

"I think his wife had a little, but nothing much."

"We shall have to go see her. But they live so terribly far away."

"Far away from you, you mean. Everything is far away from your place."

"You see, he never can forgive my living on the other side of the river," Peter Ivanovich said, smiling at Shebek. Then, still talking about how far apart different parts of the city were, they returned to the court.

Besides thinking about the promotions and transfers Ivan Ilych's death might create, everyone who heard the news felt the usual secret relief that comes when a nearby death is still someone else's death: "It is he who is dead, not I."

Each man thought or felt, "Well, he is dead, but I am alive." Ivan's closer acquaintances, the people who counted as his friends, also had a second thought: now they would have to perform the tiresome duties of propriety. They would have to attend the funeral service and pay a condolence visit to the widow. In this society, grief has a schedule and a correct appearance.

Fedor Vasilievich and Peter Ivanovich had been Ivan's nearest acquaintances. Peter Ivanovich had studied law with Ivan Ilych and considered himself somewhat indebted to him.

At dinner, Peter Ivanovich told his wife about Ivan Ilych's death and about the possible chance of getting her brother transferred into their circuit. Then he gave up his usual nap, put on his evening clothes, and drove to Ivan Ilych's house.

At the entrance stood a carriage and two cabs. Downstairs in the hall, near the cloakstand, a coffin lid leaned against the wall. It was covered in cloth of gold, decorated with gold cord and tassels, and polished with metal powder. Two women in black were taking off their fur cloaks. Peter Ivanovich recognized one as Ivan Ilych's sister; the other was a stranger. His colleague Schwartz was coming downstairs, but when he saw Peter Ivanovich enter, he stopped and winked at him, as if to say, "Ivan Ilych has made a mess of things, unlike you and me."

Schwartz had Piccadilly whiskers, a slim figure, and evening dress. As usual, he looked elegantly solemn, but this solemnity was mixed with his playful character. Here, at a funeral house, that mixture had a special sharpness, at least to Peter Ivanovich.

Peter Ivanovich let the ladies go ahead and followed them slowly upstairs. Schwartz did not go down; he stayed where he was. Peter Ivanovich understood that Schwartz wanted to arrange where they would play bridge that evening. The ladies went upstairs to the widow's room. Schwartz, with serious lips but playful eyes, pointed with his eyebrows toward the room on the right, where the body lay.

Peter Ivanovich entered, feeling the uncertainty everyone feels on these occasions: what exactly is one supposed to do? All he knew was that at a funeral it is always safe to cross oneself. He was not sure whether he should bow while doing it, so he chose a middle course. As he entered, he began crossing himself and made a slight movement like a bow. At the same time, as much as the motion of his head and hand allowed, he looked around the room. Two young men, apparently nephews, one of them a high-school student, were leaving and crossing themselves. An old woman stood motionless. A lady with strangely arched eyebrows whispered something to her. A strong, decisive Church Reader in a frock coat read aloud in a tone that allowed no disagreement. Gerasim, the butler's assistant, stepped lightly in front of Peter Ivanovich, scattering something on the floor. At once Peter Ivanovich noticed the faint smell of a decomposing body.

The last time Peter Ivanovich had visited Ivan Ilych, he had seen Gerasim in the study. Ivan had been especially fond of him, and Gerasim had been helping nurse him.

Peter Ivanovich kept crossing himself, slightly inclining his head somewhere between the coffin, the Reader, and the icons on the table in the corner. When it seemed to him that the motion of crossing himself had gone on too long, he stopped and began to look at the corpse.

The dead man lay as dead men always seem to lie: with a special heaviness, his stiff limbs sunk into the soft cushions of the coffin and his head bowed forever on the pillow. His yellow, waxy forehead, with bald patches over the sunken temples, was thrust upward in the peculiar way of the dead. His protruding nose seemed to press down on the upper lip. He had changed greatly and had become even thinner since Peter Ivanovich last saw him. But, as often happens with the dead, his face looked handsomer and, above all, more dignified than when he was alive. Its expression seemed to say that what needed to be done had been done, and done correctly. It also carried a reproach and a warning to the living. Peter Ivanovich felt that the warning was out of place, or at least that it did not apply to him. It made him uncomfortable. So he quickly crossed himself again and turned to leave, too quickly and too carelessly for proper etiquette, as he himself knew.

Schwartz was waiting in the next room, standing with his legs spread wide and both hands playing with his top hat behind his back. Just the sight of that playful, well-groomed, elegant figure refreshed Peter Ivanovich. He felt that Schwartz was above all this and would not surrender to anything depressing. Schwartz's whole expression seemed to say that a church service for Ivan Ilych was not enough reason to disturb the regular order of the evening. In plain terms: Ivan's death would certainly not prevent them from opening a fresh pack of cards, shuffling them, and playing bridge by candlelight. There was no reason to suppose the evening could not still be pleasant. Schwartz even whispered this as Peter Ivanovich passed, suggesting they meet for a game at Fedor Vasilievich's. But apparently Peter Ivanovich was not meant to play bridge that evening. Praskovya Fedorovna came out of her room with some other ladies. She was short and stout, and though she had plainly tried to resist it, she had continued to widen steadily from the shoulders downward. She wore black, with lace on her head, and had the same extraordinary arched eyebrows as the lady by the coffin. She led the ladies toward the room where the body lay and said, "The service will begin immediately. Please go in."

Schwartz made an ambiguous bow and stayed where he was, neither clearly accepting nor refusing the invitation. Praskovya Fedorovna recognized Peter Ivanovich, sighed, came close to him, took his hand, and said, "I know you were a true friend to Ivan Ilych..." Then she looked at him, waiting for the correct response. Peter Ivanovich knew that just as crossing himself had been the correct thing in the other room, the correct thing here was to press her hand, sigh, and say, "Believe me..." So he did it all, and as he did, he felt that the desired effect had been achieved: both he and she appeared touched.

"Come with me. I want to speak to you before it begins," said the widow. "Give me your arm."

Peter Ivanovich gave her his arm, and they went toward the inner rooms, passing Schwartz, who winked at him with compassion.

"There goes our bridge. Don't object if we find another player. Perhaps you can cut in when you escape," Schwartz's playful look seemed to say.

Peter Ivanovich sighed even more deeply and sadly, and Praskovya Fedorovna pressed his arm gratefully. They reached the drawing room, upholstered in pink cretonne and lit by a dim lamp. She sat on a sofa, and Peter Ivanovich sat on a low pouffe whose springs lurched under his weight. Praskovya Fedorovna was about to warn him to choose another seat, but decided that such practical advice did not fit her current role as grieving widow. As he sat down, Peter Ivanovich remembered how Ivan Ilych had arranged this room and had consulted him about the pink cretonne with green leaves. The room was crowded with furniture and ornaments. On the way to the sofa, the lace of the widow's black shawl caught on the edge of the table. Peter Ivanovich rose to free it, and when his weight lifted, the springs of the pouffe rose too and pushed at him. The widow began freeing the shawl herself. Peter Ivanovich sat again, suppressing the rebellious springs beneath him. But she had not quite freed herself, so he got up again, and again the pouffe rebelled and even creaked. When it was finally over, she took out a clean cambric handkerchief and began to cry. The whole business with the shawl and the pouffe had cooled Peter Ivanovich's emotions, and he sat there with a sullen look. This awkward moment was interrupted by Sokolov, Ivan Ilych's butler, who came to report that the cemetery plot Praskovya Fedorovna had chosen would cost two hundred rubles. She stopped crying and, looking at Peter Ivanovich like a victim, said in French that it was very hard for her. The French matters here: she is performing refined suffering in the language of upper-class polish. Peter Ivanovich made a silent gesture showing that, yes, he fully believed it must be hard.

"Please smoke," she said in a generous but crushed voice, then turned to discuss the price of the grave plot with Sokolov.

While lighting his cigarette, Peter Ivanovich heard her ask in great detail about the prices of different cemetery plots and finally decide which one she would take. After that she gave instructions about hiring the choir. Sokolov left.

"I look after everything myself," she told Peter Ivanovich, shifting the albums on the table. Then, noticing that his cigarette ash threatened the table, she immediately handed him an ashtray and said, "I consider it an affectation to say that my grief prevents me from attending to practical affairs. On the contrary, if anything can -- I will not say console me, but distract me -- it is seeing to everything concerning him." She took out her handkerchief again as if preparing to cry, but suddenly, as if mastering her feeling, shook herself and began to speak calmly. "But there is something I want to talk to you about."

Peter Ivanovich bowed, while keeping control of the pouffe springs, which immediately began trembling under him.

"He suffered terribly the last few days."

"Did he?" said Peter Ivanovich.

"Oh, terribly! He screamed without stopping, not for minutes but for hours. For the last three days he screamed incessantly. It was unendurable. I cannot understand how I bore it. You could hear him three rooms away. Oh, what I have suffered!"

"Is it possible he was conscious all that time?" Peter Ivanovich asked.

"Yes," she whispered. "To the last moment. He said goodbye to us a quarter of an hour before he died and asked us to take Volodya away."

The thought of this man's suffering struck Peter Ivanovich with horror. Ivan had been someone he knew intimately: first a cheerful little boy, then a schoolmate, then a grown colleague. Despite his unpleasant awareness that both he and the widow were performing their feelings, Peter Ivanovich suddenly saw again the dead forehead and the nose pressing down on the lip, and he became afraid for himself.

"Three days of terrible suffering, and then death! Why, that could suddenly happen to me at any time," he thought, and for a moment he was terrified. But somehow, without even knowing how, the usual protective thought immediately came: this happened to Ivan Ilych, not to me. It should not and could not happen to me. To think otherwise would be to give in to depression, and Schwartz's expression had made it clear that one should not do that. With that thought, Peter Ivanovich felt reassured and began asking about the details of Ivan Ilych's death with interest, as if death were a natural accident for Ivan Ilych but certainly not for himself.

After many details of Ivan's truly dreadful physical suffering -- details Peter Ivanovich understood mainly through the effect they had had on Praskovya Fedorovna's nerves -- the widow apparently felt it was time to get to business.

"Oh, Peter Ivanovich, how hard it is! How terribly, terribly hard!" And she began to cry again.

Peter Ivanovich sighed and waited for her to finish blowing her nose. When she had done so, he said, "Believe me..." and she began talking again. Now she brought out what was clearly her real reason for speaking with him: she wanted to know how she could obtain a grant of money from the government after her husband's death. She made it seem as if she were asking Peter Ivanovich for advice about her pension, but he soon saw that she already knew the matter down to the smallest detail, even more than he did. She knew how much could be obtained from the government because of her husband's death, but wanted to find out whether there was any way to extract more. Peter Ivanovich tried to think of a way. After reflecting for a while and, for propriety's sake, condemning the government's stinginess, he said he thought nothing more could be obtained. Then she sighed and clearly began thinking of how to get rid of her visitor. Seeing this, he put out his cigarette, rose, pressed her hand, and went into the anteroom.

In the dining room, where stood the clock Ivan Ilych had liked so much and had bought at an antique shop, Peter Ivanovich met a priest and a few acquaintances who had come for the service. He recognized Ivan Ilych's daughter, a handsome young woman in black. Her slim figure looked even slimmer, and her expression was gloomy, determined, almost angry. She bowed to Peter Ivanovich as if he were somehow to blame. Behind her stood a wealthy young man, an examining magistrate whom Peter Ivanovich knew and had heard was her fiance. He wore the same offended look. Peter Ivanovich bowed mournfully to them and was about to pass into the death room when, from under the stairs, appeared Ivan Ilych's schoolboy son, who looked extremely like his father. He seemed like a little Ivan Ilych, the kind Peter Ivanovich remembered from law school. His tear-stained eyes had the look seen in boys of thirteen or fourteen who are not innocent in the simple childish way anymore. When he saw Peter Ivanovich, he scowled, morose and ashamed. Peter Ivanovich nodded to him and entered the death room.

The service began: candles, groans, incense, tears, and sobs. Peter Ivanovich stood looking gloomily down at his feet. He did not look once at the dead man. He did not yield to any depressing influence. He was one of the first to leave.

No one was in the anteroom, but Gerasim came quickly out of the dead man's room, rummaged with his strong hands among the fur coats, found Peter Ivanovich's, and helped him into it.

"Well, friend Gerasim," said Peter Ivanovich, just to say something. "It is a sad affair, isn't it?"

"It is God's will. We shall all come to it some day," said Gerasim. He showed his even white teeth, the teeth of a healthy peasant, and, like a man in the middle of urgent work, briskly opened the front door, called the coachman, helped Peter Ivanovich into the sledge, and sprang back to the porch as if ready for the next thing he had to do.

After the smell of incense, corpse, and carbolic acid, the fresh air felt especially pleasant to Peter Ivanovich.

"Where to, sir?" asked the coachman.

"It is not too late even now... I will call on Fedor Vasilievich."

So he drove there and found them just finishing the first rubber of cards, which made it very convenient for him to cut in.

## Chapter II: A Correct Life Takes Shape

### Before you read

Before reading: this chapter explains how Ivan's life became 'correct.' Words like propriety, decorum, pleasantness, and comme il faut are load-bearing. They mean socially approved form, not deep goodness.

The French phrase 'Il faut que jeunesse se passe' means roughly 'youth must have its fling.' Tolstoy uses it to show how society excuses self-indulgence when it is done by the right people in the right style.

### Jerry Edition (direct rewrite across the full novella; preserves source order and important quotes)

Ivan Ilych's life had been very simple, very ordinary, and therefore very terrible.

He had been a member of the Court of Justice and died at forty-five. His father had been a government official in Petersburg. He had served in various ministries and departments and had made the kind of career that ends in a strange official limbo: men like him are too senior to dismiss, even when everyone knows they are no longer fit for any real responsibility. So special posts are invented for them. The posts are mostly fictional; the salaries, six to ten thousand rubles, are not fictional. With those salaries, they live on into old age.

That was Ilya Epimovich Golovin: Privy Councillor, and a superfluous member of several superfluous institutions.

He had three sons. Ivan Ilych was the second. The eldest son was following the same path as his father, just in a different department, and was already approaching the stage where he too would reach some comfortable useless post. The youngest son was considered a failure. He had spoiled his prospects in several positions and was now working in the railway department. His father and brothers disliked meeting him, and their wives disliked it even more. Unless forced to remember him, they preferred to act as if he did not exist. Ivan's sister had married Baron Greff, another Petersburg official of the same general type as her father. Ivan Ilych, people said, was "le phenix de la famille," the phoenix of the family: the exceptional one, the successful one, the family member who seemed to rise above the others.

Ivan was not as cold and stiff as his elder brother, and not as unruly as the younger. He was the pleasant middle case: intelligent, polished, lively, and agreeable. He had studied with his younger brother at the School of Law, but the younger brother failed to finish and was expelled in the fifth class. Ivan finished well. Even as a student he was already what he would remain for the rest of his life: capable, cheerful, good-natured, sociable, and strict about whatever he considered his duty. The catch is that he understood "duty" as whatever people in authority considered duty. He was not exactly a flatterer, either as a boy or as a man. But from early youth he was naturally drawn to high-ranking people the way a fly is drawn to light. He absorbed their manners, their opinions, their view of life, and made friendly connections with them. Childhood enthusiasms and youthful ideals passed through him without leaving much mark. He gave in to sensual pleasure, vanity, and later a mild fashionable liberalism, but always within the limits his instinct told him were acceptable.

At school he had done things that once seemed disgusting to him and made him feel ashamed of himself. But later, when he saw that people of good position did the same things and did not consider them wrong, he managed not exactly to call them right, but to forget them or stop being troubled by the memory.

After graduating from the School of Law, qualifying for the tenth rank of the civil service, and receiving money from his father for his equipment, Ivan Ilych arranged his entrance into adult life like a proper young official. He ordered clothes from Scharmer, the fashionable tailor. He hung a medallion on his watch-chain inscribed "respice finem," meaning "look to the end," which is bitterly ironic because he will spend most of his life avoiding the end. He said goodbye to his professor and to the prince who patronized the school. He had a farewell dinner with his classmates at Donon's, a first-class restaurant. Then, with a fashionable new travelling bag, linen, clothes, shaving tools, toilet articles, and a travelling rug, all bought from the best shops, he set off for a province where his father's influence had gotten him attached to the governor as an official for special service.

In the province, Ivan Ilych quickly arranged for himself a life as easy and agreeable as the one he had enjoyed at the School of Law. He performed his official duties, advanced his career, and amused himself in a way that remained pleasant and respectable. Sometimes he made official visits to country districts. There he behaved with dignity toward both superiors and inferiors, and he carried out the duties entrusted to him, mainly involving religious sectarians, with an exactness and incorruptible honesty that he could not help feeling proud of.

In official matters, despite his youth and his taste for lighthearted pleasure, he was reserved, precise, and even severe. In society, however, he was amusing and witty, always good-natured and correct. The governor and his wife, who treated him almost like one of the family, called him "bon enfant," a good fellow.

In the province he had an affair with a lady who made advances to the elegant young lawyer. There was also a milliner. There were drinking parties with aides-de-camp who visited the district, and after-supper visits to a questionable outlying street. There was also some submissive politeness toward his chief and even toward his chief's wife. But all of it was done in such a well-bred tone that no harsh name could be attached to it. It all fell under the French saying "Il faut que jeunesse se passe": youth must have its fling. Tolstoy's point is not that Ivan was uniquely wicked. It is worse than that. His self-indulgence was washed clean by linen, manners, French phrases, and the approval of people with rank.

Ivan Ilych served this way for five years. Then came a change in his official life. New reformed judicial institutions were introduced, and new men were needed. Ivan became one of those new men. He was offered the post of examining magistrate. He accepted, even though it meant moving to another province, giving up the connections he had already formed, and making new ones. His friends gave him a send-off. They took a group photograph, presented him with a silver cigarette case, and he went to his new post.

As an examining magistrate, Ivan Ilych was just as comme il faut, just as proper by elite social rules, as he had been in his earlier special-service post. He inspired general respect and knew how to keep official duties separate from private life. But the new duties were far more interesting and attractive. In his old position, it had been pleasant to wear an undress uniform made by Scharmer and walk past the crowd of petitioners and officials who waited nervously to see the governor. They envied him as he walked freely into his chief's private room for tea and a cigarette. Still, not many people had actually depended on him then: mostly police officials and sectarians during special missions. He liked treating them politely, almost like comrades, as if he were letting them feel that he, the man with power over them, was choosing to be friendly.

Now, as examining magistrate, the power was much broader. Ivan Ilych felt that everyone, even important and self-satisfied people, could fall under his authority. He had only to write a few words on a paper with the right heading, and some important person would be summoned before him as an accused person or a witness. If Ivan did not invite him to sit, that person would have to stand and answer questions. Ivan never abused this power. On the contrary, he tried to soften its expression. But the awareness that he possessed the power, and the ability to soften it, became the main charm of his work. In the work itself, especially in examinations, he quickly learned how to strip away anything that did not belong to the legal form of the case. Even a complicated matter could be reduced to what would appear on paper: external facts, no personal opinion, and every prescribed formality observed. The work was new, and Ivan Ilych was among the first to apply the new Code of 1864.

When he took up the post of examining magistrate in a new town, he formed new acquaintances and connections. He placed himself on a new footing and adopted a slightly different tone. Toward the provincial authorities he maintained a dignified distance. From the legal gentlemen and wealthy gentry in town he selected the best circle. In that circle he took on the attitude of a moderately dissatisfied, moderately liberal, enlightened citizen. At the same time, without changing the elegance of his clothing, he stopped shaving his chin and let his beard grow as it pleased.

Ivan Ilych settled very pleasantly into this new town. The society there leaned against the governor, which suited his moderate opposition. His salary was larger. He began to play whist, which added a great deal to his pleasure in life. He had a talent for cards: he played good-humoredly, counted quickly and shrewdly, and usually won.

After two years there he met his future wife, Praskovya Fedorovna Mikhel. She was the most attractive, clever, and brilliant girl in the circle he moved in. Among his other amusements and relaxations from official labor, Ivan established a light and playful relation with her.

When he had been an official for special service, Ivan had often danced. Now, as an examining magistrate, dancing was exceptional. If he danced, he did it as if to show that although he belonged to the reformed judicial order and had reached the fifth official rank, he could still dance better than most people. So at the end of an evening he sometimes danced with Praskovya Fedorovna, and it was mainly during these dances that he captivated her. She fell in love with him. Ivan Ilych had not originally meant to marry her, but when she fell in love with him, he said to himself, "Really, why shouldn't I marry?"

Praskovya Fedorovna came from a good family, was not bad-looking, and had a little property. Ivan might have aimed at a more brilliant match, but this one was good enough. He had his salary, and he hoped she would have an equal income. She was well connected, sweet, pretty, and completely correct. It would be wrong to say Ivan married because he was in love with her and found that she shared his view of life. It would also be wrong to say he married only because his social circle approved of the match. Both motives worked on him. The marriage gave him personal satisfaction, and it was also considered the right thing by the people whose approval mattered most.

So Ivan Ilych got married.

The preparations for marriage and the beginning of married life were pleasant: conjugal affection, new furniture, new dishes, new linen. At first Ivan began to think marriage would not damage the easy, agreeable, cheerful, and always decorous life that society approved of and that he regarded as natural. Maybe marriage would even improve it. But from the first months of his wife's pregnancy, something new appeared: something unpleasant, depressing, improper, and impossible to escape.

His wife, for no reason, "de gaiete de coeur" as Ivan put it to himself, began disturbing the pleasure and propriety of their life. The French phrase means something like "out of sheer high spirits" or "for no serious reason," but Ivan uses it to make her suffering and anger sound irrational and almost frivolous. She became jealous without cause, demanded his full attention, found fault with everything, and made scenes that seemed coarse and ill-mannered to him.

At first Ivan tried to escape this unpleasantness through the same easy and decorous relation to life that had always worked for him. He ignored his wife's disagreeable moods, continued his usual pleasant life, invited friends over for cards, and tried going out to his club or spending evenings with friends. But one day she attacked him so fiercely, with such coarse words, and continued to abuse him every time he did not meet her demands, with such obvious determination not to yield until he submitted, meaning until he stayed home and was bored as she was, that he became alarmed. He realized that marriage, at least with Praskovya Fedorovna, did not always support the pleasures and amenities of life. It often violated comfort and propriety. Therefore he would have to fortify himself against it. His official duties were the one thing that impressed Praskovya Fedorovna, so he used those duties to struggle for his independence.

Then came the child: attempts to feed it, failures in feeding it, real and imagined illnesses of mother and child, and demands for Ivan's sympathy in matters he did not understand. All of this made the need for a life outside the family even more urgent.

As his wife became more irritable and demanding, Ivan shifted the center of gravity of his life more and more into official work. The more he did this, the more he liked his work and the more ambitious he became.

Very soon, within a year of the wedding, Ivan Ilych understood marriage in his own way. Marriage might add some comforts to life, but it was also a complicated and difficult affair. To perform one's duty in marriage, that is, to lead a decorous life approved by society, one had to adopt a definite attitude toward it, just as one adopted a definite attitude toward official duties.

So Ivan created an attitude toward married life. He required from marriage only the conveniences it could provide: dinner at home, a housewife, a bed, and above all the proper outward form required by public opinion. For everything else, he looked for lighthearted pleasure and propriety. When he found them at home, he was grateful. When he met antagonism and complaints, he withdrew at once into his fenced-off world of official duties, where he found satisfaction.

Ivan Ilych was considered a good official, and after three years he became Assistant Public Prosecutor. His new duties made his work even more attractive: their importance, the possibility of indicting and imprisoning anyone he chose, the publicity of his speeches, and his success in all these things.

More children were born. His wife became more and more irritable and bad-tempered. But the attitude Ivan had adopted toward home life made him almost immune to her grumbling.

After seven years in that town, he was transferred to another province as Public Prosecutor. They moved, but they were short of money and his wife disliked the new place. Although the salary was higher, the cost of living was higher too. Two of their children died, and family life became still more unpleasant.

Praskovya Fedorovna blamed her husband for every inconvenience in the new home. Most conversations between husband and wife, especially about the children's education, led back to old disputes, and the disputes could flare up at any moment. There remained only rare periods of affection. These were like small islands where they anchored briefly before setting out again onto an ocean of hidden hostility, shown by their distance from each other. That distance might have grieved Ivan if he had thought it was wrong. But by now he considered it normal and even made it his goal in family life. He wanted to free himself more and more from domestic unpleasantness, while giving the whole arrangement an appearance of harmlessness and propriety. He did this by spending less and less time with his family, and when he had to be home, protecting himself by the presence of outsiders.

The main thing, though, was his official work. The whole interest of his life now centered there, and that interest absorbed him. The awareness of power, the ability to ruin anyone he wished to ruin, the importance and external dignity of entering court or meeting subordinates, his success with superiors and inferiors, and above all his skill in handling cases, which he knew he possessed, all gave him pleasure and filled his life. Alongside conversations with colleagues, dinners, and bridge, it was enough. On the whole, Ivan Ilych's life continued to flow as he believed it should: pleasantly and properly.

So things continued for another seven years. His eldest daughter was already sixteen. Another child had died. Only one son remained, a schoolboy and a source of disagreement. Ivan wanted to put him in the School of Law, but Praskovya Fedorovna, to spite him, entered him in the High School. The daughter had been educated at home and had turned out well. The boy did not do badly either.

## Chapter III: Promotion, House, and the Fall

### Before you read

Ivan Ilych lived like this for seventeen years after his marriage. By then he had long been a Public Prosecutor. He had turned down several possible transfers because he was waiting for a better post. Then something unexpected and unpleasant upset the smooth course of his life. He expected to be offered the position of presiding judge in a university town, but somehow Hoppe came forward and got the appointment instead. Ivan became irritable. He blamed Hoppe, quarreled with him, and quarreled with his immediate superiors too. They became colder toward him and passed him over again when other appointments were made.

This was 1880, the hardest year of Ivan Ilych's life. Two things became clear to him at once. First, his salary was not enough for the life he and his family were trying to live. Second, he had been forgotten. Worse than that, what seemed to him the greatest and cruelest injustice appeared to everyone else to be an ordinary career inconvenience. Even his father did not think he had a duty to help. Ivan felt abandoned by everyone. Other people treated his position, with a salary of 3,500 rubles, as normal and even fortunate. Only he knew that it was not normal at all: he felt the injustice done to him, heard his wife's constant nagging, and carried debts created by living beyond his means.

To save money that summer, he took leave from work and went with his wife to stay in the country at her brother's place.

In the country, without his work, Ivan experienced boredom for the first time in his life. It was not just ordinary boredom. It was unbearable depression. He decided he could not go on living like that and had to take energetic action.

After a sleepless night pacing up and down the veranda, he decided to go to Petersburg and stir things up. He would punish the people who had failed to appreciate him and get himself transferred to another ministry.

The next day, despite protests from his wife and her brother, he set out for Petersburg with one object: to obtain a post with a salary of five thousand rubles a year. He was no longer attached to any particular department, political tendency, or kind of work. Administration, banks, railways, one of the Empress Marya's Institutions, customs -- any of them would do. The point was not the work. The point was five thousand rubles, and a ministry other than the one that had failed to appreciate him.

Ivan Ilych's quest was unexpectedly successful. At Kursk, an acquaintance named F. I. Ilyin entered the first-class carriage and sat beside him. Ilyin told him about a telegram just received by the governor of Kursk: a change was about to happen in the ministry. Peter Ivanovich would be replaced by Ivan Semyonovich.

This proposed change mattered for Russia, but it mattered even more for Ivan Ilych. A new man at the top would bring forward Peter Petrovich, and that would bring forward Peter Petrovich's friend Zakhar Ivanovich. Zakhar Ivanovich was Ivan Ilych's friend and colleague. In other words, the great machinery of government had suddenly shifted in a way that might rescue Ivan personally.

In Moscow the news was confirmed. When Ivan reached Petersburg, he found Zakhar Ivanovich and received a definite promise of an appointment in his former Department of Justice.

A week later he telegraphed his wife: "Zakhar in Miller's place. I shall receive appointment on presentation of report."

Because of this change in personnel, Ivan unexpectedly obtained an appointment in his old ministry that placed him two ranks above his former colleagues. It gave him a salary of five thousand rubles and another three thousand five hundred for moving expenses. All his anger toward his former enemies and the whole department disappeared. Ivan Ilych was completely happy.

He returned to the country more cheerful and contented than he had been for a long time. Praskovya Fedorovna cheered up too, and they arranged a truce. Ivan told her how everyone in Petersburg had celebrated him, how the enemies who had failed to appreciate him had been put to shame and now fawned on him, how envious they were of his appointment, and how much everyone in Petersburg had liked him.

Praskovya Fedorovna listened and seemed to believe it. She did not contradict him. Instead she began making plans for their life in the town where they were going. Ivan was delighted to see that her plans matched his plans. He and his wife were in agreement. After this stumble, life seemed to be regaining its proper natural character: pleasant, lighthearted, and decorous.

Ivan Ilych had come back only briefly. He had to take up his new duties on September 10. He also needed time to settle into the new place, move all their belongings from the province, and buy or order many additional things. In short, he had to arrange the life he had resolved on, almost exactly as Praskovya Fedorovna had resolved on it too.

Because everything had turned out so fortunately, because he and his wife wanted the same things, and because they would not be seeing too much of each other, they got along better than they had since the first years of marriage. Ivan had thought of taking his family with him immediately, but his wife's brother and sister-in-law suddenly became very amiable and friendly and urged him to go alone first. He agreed.

So he went off alone. His cheerful mood, produced by success and by temporary harmony with his wife, did not leave him. He found a delightful house, exactly the kind he and his wife had dreamed of: spacious, high-ceilinged reception rooms in the old style, a convenient and dignified study for himself, rooms for his wife and daughter, and a study for his son. It was as if the house had been built specially for them.

Ivan supervised the arrangements himself. He chose the wallpapers, added furniture, especially antiques that he considered particularly comme il faut -- proper, tasteful, and socially correct -- and oversaw the upholstery. Everything progressed toward the ideal he had set in his mind. Even half-finished, the rooms exceeded his expectations. He could already see the refined, elegant, non-vulgar character the place would have when ready. As he fell asleep, he pictured the reception room. Looking at the unfinished drawing room, he could already imagine the fireplace, the screen, the what-not cabinet, the small chairs placed here and there, the dishes and plates on the walls, and the bronzes, all in their proper positions. He was pleased to think how impressed his wife and daughter would be, since they shared his taste. They certainly were not expecting anything this good. He had been especially successful, he thought, in finding and cheaply buying antiques that gave the whole place an aristocratic character. In his letters he deliberately understated everything so he could surprise them.

All this absorbed him so completely that his new duties, though he liked official work, interested him less than he had expected. Sometimes during court sessions he became absent-minded and wondered whether the curtain cornices should be straight or curved. He was so interested in the arrangements that he often did things himself, moving furniture or rehanging curtains. Once, while climbing a step-ladder to show the upholsterer how he wanted the hangings draped, he made a false step and slipped. Since he was strong and agile, he caught himself and only struck his side against the knob of the window frame. The bruise hurt, but the pain soon passed. Just then he felt especially bright and healthy. He wrote, "I feel fifteen years younger."

He thought everything would be ready by September, but the work dragged on until mid-October. Still, the result was charming, not only to him but to everyone who saw it.

In reality, the house was exactly what one usually sees in the homes of people of moderate means who want to look rich. That is why such homes end up resembling one another: damasks, dark wood, plants, rugs, dull and polished bronzes, all the things people of that class buy in order to resemble other people of that class. Ivan's house was so much like the others that no one would have noticed it. But to him it seemed exceptional. This is the important turn: the house feels unique to Ivan precisely because he has perfectly copied the taste of his class.

He was very happy when he met his family at the station and brought them to the newly furnished house, all lit up. A footman in a white tie opened the door into the hall decorated with plants. They went into the drawing room and the study, exclaiming with delight. Ivan led them everywhere, drank in their praise, and beamed with pleasure. At tea that evening, when Praskovya Fedorovna asked about his fall, he laughed and showed them how he had gone flying and frightened the upholsterer.

"It is a good thing I am a bit of an athlete," he said. "Another man might have been killed, but I only knocked myself here. It hurts when touched, but it is passing already. Only a bruise."

So they began living in the new home. As always happens after a family settles into a house, they discovered they were just one room short. And with the increased income, as always happens, they were still just a little short -- about five hundred rubles short. But everything was very nice.

Things went especially well at first, while arrangements were still unfinished and there was always something left to do: something to buy, something to order, something to move, something to adjust. Husband and wife still had disputes, but they were both so pleased and so busy that the disputes did not become serious quarrels. When nothing remained to arrange, life became a little dull and seemed to be missing something. But by then they were making acquaintances, forming habits, and life was filling out again.

Ivan Ilych spent his mornings at the law court and came home to dinner. At first he was usually in a good mood, though he sometimes became irritable precisely because of the house. Every spot on the tablecloth or upholstery, every broken blind cord, annoyed him. He had put so much effort into arranging everything that any disturbance of it distressed him. But overall his life ran as he believed life should run: easily, pleasantly, and decorously.

He got up at nine, drank coffee, read the paper, put on his undress uniform, and went to court. There the harness of work already fit him perfectly. He slipped into it without difficulty: petitioners, inquiries at the chancery, the chancery itself, public sittings, administrative sittings. The key in all of it was to exclude anything fresh and living, because anything fresh and living disturbed the regular flow of official business. Only official relations with people were admitted, and only on official grounds.

For example, if a man came wanting information, Ivan Ilych would have nothing to do with him if the matter did not fall within his official sphere. But if the man had business with him in an official capacity, something that could be written on stamped paper, Ivan would do everything he could within those limits. While doing it, he preserved the appearance of friendly human relations; that is, he observed the courtesies of life. But as soon as the official relation ended, everything else ended too.

Ivan possessed this ability to separate the official side of life from real human life to the highest degree. Through long practice and natural talent, he became so skilled that, like a virtuoso, he sometimes allowed the human and official relations to mingle a little. He allowed this only because he knew he could resume the strictly official attitude whenever he chose and drop the human relation at once. He did all of it easily, pleasantly, correctly, and even artistically.

Between sessions he smoked, drank tea, and chatted a little about politics, a little about general matters, a little about cards, and most of all about official appointments. Then, tired but satisfied like a first violinist who has played his part in an orchestra precisely, he returned home. His wife and daughter might have been out paying calls or receiving visitors. His son had been to school, had done his homework with his tutor, and was surely learning what boys learn in high school. Everything was as it should be.

After dinner, if there were no visitors, Ivan sometimes read a book that people were discussing. In the evening he settled down to work: reading official papers, comparing witness depositions, and marking the relevant paragraphs of the Code. This was neither dull nor amusing. It was dull when he might have been playing bridge. But if bridge was not available, it was better than doing nothing or sitting with his wife.

Ivan's favorite domestic pleasure was giving little dinners to socially suitable men and women. Just as his drawing room resembled every other drawing room of its type, his enjoyable little parties resembled every other enjoyable little party of their class.

Once they even gave a dance. Ivan enjoyed it, and everything went well except that it led to a violent quarrel with his wife over the cakes and sweets. Praskovya Fedorovna had made her own plans, but Ivan insisted on ordering everything from an expensive confectioner. He ordered too many cakes, and the quarrel broke out because some were left over and the confectioner's bill came to forty-five rubles. It was a large and unpleasant quarrel. Praskovya Fedorovna called him a fool and an imbecile. He clutched his head and angrily hinted at divorce.

But the dance itself had been enjoyable. The best people were there, and Ivan had danced with Princess Trufonova, sister of the distinguished founder of the Society "Bear My Burden."

The pleasures of his work were pleasures of ambition. His social pleasures were pleasures of vanity. But Ivan Ilych's greatest pleasure was bridge. He admitted that whatever unpleasant thing happened in his life, one pleasure shone above everything like a ray of light: to sit down to bridge with good players, not noisy partners, and of course to four-handed bridge. With five players it was annoying to sit out, even though one pretended not to mind. He loved to play a clever and serious game, when the cards allowed it, and then eat supper and drink a glass of wine. After bridge, especially if he had won a little -- winning a large sum was unpleasant -- Ivan went to bed in an especially good mood.

So they lived. They formed a circle of acquaintances among the best people. Important people and young people visited them. Husband, wife, and daughter were completely agreed about whom to know. Without openly saying it, they kept at a distance the shabby friends and relatives who came gushing into the drawing room, showing too much affection among the Japanese plates on the walls. Soon these shabby friends stopped forcing themselves in, and only the best people remained in the Golovin circle.

Young men paid attention to Lisa. Petrishchev, an examining magistrate and the only heir of Dmitri Ivanovich Petrishchev, became so attentive to her that Ivan had already spoken with Praskovya Fedorovna about it. Perhaps they should arrange a party for them, or put on some private theatricals.

So they lived. Everything went well, nothing changed, and life flowed pleasantly.

They were all in good health. At first, it could not really be called illness when Ivan Ilych sometimes said he had a strange taste in his mouth and some discomfort in his left side.

But the discomfort increased. It was not exactly pain yet, but it became a pressure in his side, and it came with bad temper. For all of this he blamed Praskovya Fedorovna.

After one scene in which Ivan had been especially unfair, he explained that yes, he was irritable, but only because he was not well. Praskovya Fedorovna said that if he was ill, then it should be attended to, and she insisted that he go see a famous doctor.

He went. Everything happened as he had expected, and as it always happens. There was the usual waiting. There was the doctor's important manner, a manner Ivan knew very well because it resembled the manner he himself used in court. There were the soundings and listenings, the questions that seemed to have predetermined answers, and the look of professional importance that said: if only you put yourself in our hands, we know how to arrange everything; we know the proper method, always the same way for everyone. It was just like the law courts. The doctor took toward Ivan the same attitude Ivan took toward an accused person.

The doctor said that one sign indicated one thing inside the patient, but if an examination of something else did not confirm it, then they would have to assume another thing; and if they assumed that other thing, then still another consideration followed, and so on. For Ivan, only one question mattered: was his case serious or not? But the doctor treated that question as inappropriate. From the doctor's point of view, the real question was not Ivan's life. The real question was whether this was a floating kidney, chronic catarrh, or appendicitis. The exact disease matters less than the theater of expertise: Ivan wants to know whether he is dying; the doctor wants to solve a technical dispute between organs.

The doctor solved this dispute brilliantly, as it seemed to Ivan, in favor of the appendix, while reserving the right to reconsider if the urine analysis gave new evidence. It was exactly what Ivan himself had done brilliantly a thousand times with men on trial. The doctor summed up the case just as brilliantly, looking over his spectacles at the accused with triumph and even cheerfulness. From this summing-up, Ivan concluded that things were bad. But he also concluded that for the doctor, and probably for everyone else, it was a matter of indifference. For Ivan, it was terrible. This struck him painfully and filled him with pity for himself and bitterness toward the doctor's indifference.

He said none of this. He rose, placed the doctor's fee on the table, and said with a sigh, "We sick people probably often ask inappropriate questions. But tell me, in general, is this complaint dangerous or not?"

The doctor looked at him sternly over his spectacles with one eye, as if to say: Prisoner, if you will not confine yourself to the questions put to you, I will have to remove you from the court.

"I have already told you what I consider necessary and proper. The analysis may show something more." Then the doctor bowed.

Ivan Ilych went out slowly, sat down gloomily in his sledge, and drove home. All the way home he went over what the doctor had said, trying to translate the complicated, obscure, scientific phrases into plain language. He wanted one answer: "Is my condition bad? Is it very bad? Or is there still nothing much wrong?" It seemed to him that the real meaning of the doctor's words was that things were very bad. Everything in the streets looked depressing: cabmen, houses, passers-by, shops. His ache, the dull gnawing ache that never stopped for even a moment, now seemed to have a new and more serious meaning because of the doctor's uncertain remarks. Ivan watched it now with a new oppressive feeling.

He reached home and began telling his wife about the visit. She listened, but in the middle of his account their daughter came in with her hat on, ready to go out with her mother. The daughter sat down reluctantly to listen to the tedious story, but she could not bear it for long. Her mother also did not listen to the end.

"Well, I am very glad," Praskovya Fedorovna said. "Mind now that you take your medicine regularly. Give me the prescription and I will send Gerasim to the chemist's." Then she went to get ready to go out.

While she was in the room, Ivan had hardly had time to breathe. When she left, he sighed deeply.

"Well," he thought, "perhaps it is not so bad after all."

He began taking the medicine and following the doctor's instructions, which changed after the urine analysis. But then it turned out that the signs from the urine analysis contradicted the symptoms that appeared. What was happening did not match what the doctor had told him. Either the doctor had forgotten something, made a mistake, or hidden something from him. Still, Ivan did not blame him. He kept obeying the instructions exactly and at first found some comfort in the obedience itself.

From the time of his visit to the doctor, Ivan's main occupation became obeying medical instructions: hygiene, medicine, watching his pain, and watching his bodily functions. His chief interest became other people's illnesses and health. When sickness, death, or recovery was mentioned in his presence, especially if the illness resembled his own, he listened with hidden agitation, asked questions, and applied everything he heard to his own case.

The pain did not lessen, but Ivan tried to force himself to think he was improving. He could do that as long as nothing upset him. But as soon as he had trouble with his wife, failure at work, or bad cards at bridge, he became sharply aware of the disease. In the past he had borne such annoyances by thinking he would soon fix them, master the situation, succeed, or make a grand slam. Now every misfortune threw him into despair. He would think: "There now, just when I was beginning to get better and the medicine had begun to work, this cursed misfortune comes along." He became furious at the event or at the people causing the unpleasantness and killing him. He felt that this fury was killing him, yet he could not restrain it.

One might think it should have been clear to him that anger at circumstances and people made the illness worse, and that he should therefore ignore unpleasant events. But he drew the opposite conclusion. He said he needed peace, and then watched for everything that might disturb it, becoming irritable at the smallest violation.

His condition was made worse by reading medical books and consulting doctors. The disease advanced so gradually that when he compared one day with the next, he could deceive himself: the difference was slight. But whenever he consulted doctors, it seemed to him that he was getting worse, and getting worse quickly. Yet he kept consulting them.

That month he went to see another famous doctor. This one told him almost the same thing as the first, but asked the questions differently. The visit only increased Ivan's doubts and fears. A friend of a friend, also a very good doctor, diagnosed the illness differently from the others. Though he predicted recovery, his questions and assumptions confused Ivan even more. A homeopath diagnosed the disease in yet another way and prescribed medicine that Ivan secretly took for a week. After a week, feeling no improvement and losing confidence both in the first treatment and in this one, he became still more despondent.

One day a lady acquaintance mentioned a cure brought about by a wonder-working icon. Ivan caught himself listening closely and beginning to believe it might have happened. This frightened him. "Has my mind really weakened that much?" he asked himself. "Nonsense. It is all rubbish. I must not give in to nervous fears. I have chosen a doctor and must follow his treatment strictly. That is what I will do. Now it is settled. I will not think about it, but will follow the treatment seriously until summer, and then we shall see. No more wavering from now on."

This was easy to say and impossible to do. The pain in his side oppressed him and seemed to grow worse and more constant. The taste in his mouth became stranger. It seemed to him that his breath had a disgusting smell. His appetite and strength declined. He could no longer deceive himself: something terrible, new, and more important than anything before in his life was happening inside him. He alone was aware of it.

The people around him did not understand, or would not understand. They thought everything in the world was going on as usual. This tormented Ivan more than anything. His household, especially his wife and daughter, were caught up in a whirl of visits and did not understand. They were annoyed that he was so gloomy and demanding, as if he were to blame. Though they tried to hide it, he saw that he had become an obstacle in their path. His wife had settled on a definite attitude toward his illness, and she held to it no matter what he said or did.

Her attitude was this: "You know," she would say to friends, "Ivan Ilych cannot behave like other people and follow the treatment prescribed for him. One day he takes his drops, keeps to his diet, and goes to bed on time; the next day, unless I watch him, he forgets his medicine, eats sturgeon, which is forbidden, and sits up playing cards until one in the morning."

"Oh, come, when was that?" Ivan would ask angrily. "Only once, at Peter Ivanovich's."

"And yesterday with Shebek."

"Well, even if I had not stayed up, this pain would have kept me awake."

"Be that as it may, you will never get well like that. You will always make us miserable."

Praskovya Fedorovna's view, expressed both to others and to Ivan, was that his illness was his own fault and another annoyance he caused her. Ivan felt that this opinion slipped out of her involuntarily, but that did not make it easier to bear.

At the law courts too, Ivan noticed, or thought he noticed, a strange attitude toward him. Sometimes it seemed that people were watching him with curiosity, as a man whose post might soon be vacant. At other times his friends suddenly joked with him in a friendly way about his low spirits, as if the awful, horrible, unheard-of thing happening inside him, constantly gnawing at him and dragging him away, were an agreeable subject for jokes. Schwartz especially irritated him with his playfulness, energy, and savoir-faire, because Schwartz reminded him of what he himself had been ten years earlier.

Friends came to make up a card table, and they sat down. They dealt, bending the new cards to soften them. Ivan sorted the diamonds in his hand and found he had seven. His partner said, "No trumps," and supported him with two diamonds. What more could he want? It should have been cheerful and lively. They might make a grand slam. But suddenly Ivan felt the gnawing pain and the taste in his mouth, and it seemed absurd that in such circumstances he should care about making a grand slam.

He looked at his partner, Mikhail Mikhaylovich, who rapped the table with his strong hand. Instead of snatching up the tricks, Mikhail courteously and indulgently pushed the cards toward Ivan so Ivan could gather them without stretching out his arm. "Does he think I am too weak to stretch out my arm?" Ivan thought. Forgetting what he was doing, he over-trumped his partner and missed the grand slam by three tricks. Worst of all, he saw how upset Mikhail Mikhaylovich was about it, and he himself did not care. It was dreadful to understand why he did not care.

They all saw that he was suffering and said, "We can stop if you are tired. Take a rest."

Lie down? No, he was not tired at all. He finished the rubber. Everyone was gloomy and silent. Ivan felt that he had spread this gloom over them and could not remove it. They had supper and left. Ivan was alone with the knowledge that his life was poisoned and was poisoning the lives of others, and that the poison was not weakening but penetrating more and more deeply into his whole being.

With that knowledge, with physical pain, and with terror, he had to go to bed and often lie awake most of the night. The next morning he had to get up again, dress, go to the law courts, speak, and write. Or, if he did not go out, he had to spend at home the twenty-four hours of the day, each one a torture. And he had to live like this alone on the edge of an abyss, with no one who understood or pitied him.

"Exaggerating! Don't you see it? He is a dead man! Look at his eyes. There is no life in them. But what is wrong with him?"

"No one knows. Nikolaevich said one thing, though I don't know exactly what. Leshchetitsky, the celebrated specialist, said quite the opposite."

Ivan walked away, went to his own room, lay down, and began thinking: "The kidney, a floating kidney." He remembered what the doctors had said about the kidney detaching itself and swaying about inside him. With a desperate effort of imagination, he tried to catch that kidney, stop it, and hold it in place. It seemed to him that so little was needed. This is the trap of the medical language: it lets him picture death as if it were a small mechanical fault that could be adjusted. "No, I will go see Peter Ivanovich again." Peter Ivanovich was the friend whose friend was a doctor. Ivan rang, ordered the carriage, and got ready to go.

"Where are you going, Jean?" his wife asked, looking at him with a specially sad and unusually kind expression. "Jean" is the French-style family name for Ivan; in this house, even intimacy comes dressed in upper-class polish.

That exceptionally kind look irritated him. He looked at her gloomily.

"I must go see Peter Ivanovich."

He went to Peter Ivanovich, and together they went to Peter's doctor friend. The doctor was in, and Ivan had a long talk with him.

Going back over the anatomical and physiological details of what the doctor thought was happening inside him, Ivan understood it all.

There was something small, a little thing, in the vermiform appendix. It might all come right. Stimulate one organ, check another, then absorption would occur, evacuation would happen, and normal activity would return. He came home rather late for dinner, ate, and talked cheerfully. But for a long time he could not make himself go back to work in his room. At last he went to his study and did what had to be done, but the whole time he felt he had postponed something important and private, something he would come back to when the work was finished. When he finished, he remembered that this private matter was the thought of his appendix. But he would not surrender to it. He went to the drawing room for tea instead. There were callers, including the examining magistrate who was a desirable match for his daughter. They talked, played the piano, and sang. Ivan, as Praskovya Fedorovna noticed, spent the evening more cheerfully than usual. But not for one moment did he forget that he had postponed the important matter of the appendix.

At eleven o'clock he said good night and went to his bedroom. Since his illness, he had slept alone in a small room next to his study. He undressed and picked up a novel by Zola, but instead of reading he fell into thought. In his imagination, the improvement he wanted in the appendix began to happen. Absorption took place. Evacuation took place. Normal activity was restored. "Yes, that is it!" he said to himself. "One only has to assist nature, that is all." He remembered his medicine, got up, took it, and lay down on his back, watching for the medicine to begin its good work and lessen the pain. "I only need to take it regularly and avoid every harmful influence. I am already feeling better, much better." He touched his side. It was not painful to the touch. "There, I really do not feel it. It is much better already." He put out the light and turned on his side. "The appendix is getting better. Absorption is happening."

Then, suddenly, he felt the same old dull gnawing pain, familiar, stubborn, and serious. The same disgusting taste was in his mouth. His heart sank and his head went dim. "My God! My God!" he muttered. "Again, again! And it will never stop." Suddenly the whole matter appeared differently. "Appendix! Kidney!" he said to himself. "It is not a question of appendix or kidney. It is a question of life and death. Yes, life was here, and now it is going, going, and I cannot stop it. Why deceive myself? Isn't it obvious to everyone except me that I am dying, and that it is only a question of weeks, days, maybe this very moment? There was light, and now there is darkness. I was here, and now I am going there. Where?" A chill came over him. His breathing stopped. He felt only the beating of his heart.

"When I am not here, what will there be? There will be nothing. Then where shall I be when I no longer exist? Can this be dying? No, I do not want to!" He jumped up and tried to light the candle. His hands trembled as he felt for it. He dropped both candle and candlestick on the floor and fell back onto his pillow.

"What is the use? It makes no difference," he said to himself, staring wide-eyed into the darkness. "Death. Yes, death. And none of them knows it, and none of them wants to know it, and they have no pity for me. Now they are playing." Through the door he heard the distant sound of singing and accompaniment. "It is all the same to them. But they will die too! Fools! I first, and they later, but the same thing will happen to them. And now they are merry. The beasts!"

Anger choked him, and he was unbearably, agonizingly miserable. "It cannot be true that all men are doomed to suffer this awful horror!" He raised himself.

"Something must be wrong. I must calm myself. I must think it all over from the beginning." And he began thinking again. "Yes, the beginning of my illness: I knocked my side, but that day and the next I was still quite well. It hurt a little, then more. I saw doctors. Then came depression and anguish, more doctors, and I drew nearer to the abyss. My strength grew less and less. I kept coming nearer and nearer, and now I have wasted away and there is no light in my eyes. I think about the appendix, but this is death. I think about mending the appendix, and all the while here is death. Can it really be death?" Terror seized him again and he gasped for breath. He leaned down to feel for the matches, pressing his elbow against the stand beside the bed. The stand was in his way and hurt him. He became furious with it, pressed against it harder, and knocked it over. Breathless and desperate, he fell back on his back and expected death to come at once.

Meanwhile the visitors were leaving. Praskovya Fedorovna was seeing them off. She heard something fall and came in.

"What has happened?"

"Nothing. I knocked it over accidentally."

She went out and returned with a candle. He lay there panting heavily, like a man who had run a thousand yards, staring up at her with a fixed look.

"What is it, Jean?"

"No-o-thing. I upset it." Why talk about it? he thought. She will not understand.

And in fact she did not understand. She picked up the stand, lit his candle, and hurried away to see another visitor off. When she came back, he still lay on his back, looking upward.

"What is it? Do you feel worse?"

"Yes."

She shook her head and sat down.

"Do you know, Jean, I think we must ask Leshchetitsky to come and see you here."

That meant calling the famous specialist to the house, no matter the expense. Ivan smiled maliciously and said, "No." She stayed a little longer, then went up to him and kissed his forehead.

While she kissed him, he hated her from the bottom of his soul and could barely keep himself from pushing her away.

"Good night. Please God you will sleep."

"Yes."

## Chapter VI: Death Stops Being Abstract

### Before you read

Before reading: this short chapter is where death stops being an idea that happens to other people. Ivan can no longer keep it at the level of diagnosis or etiquette.

### Jerry Edition (direct rewrite across the full novella; preserves source order and important quotes)

Ivan Ilych saw that he was dying, and he lived in continual despair.

Deep in his heart he knew he was dying. But he was not used to the thought. More than that, he did not and could not grasp it.

He remembered the syllogism he had learned in Kiesewetter's Logic: "Caius is a man; men are mortal; therefore Caius is mortal." That had always seemed correct when it applied to Caius. It had certainly never seemed correct when applied to himself. Caius was "man" in the abstract, and of course an abstract man was mortal. But Ivan was not Caius. He was not an abstract man. He was a creature completely separate from all others. He had been little Vanya, with a mama and papa, with Mitya and Volodya, with toys, a coachman, a nurse, and later Katenka, and all the joys, griefs, and delights of childhood, boyhood, and youth. What did Caius know about the smell of the striped leather ball Vanya had loved? Had Caius kissed his mother's hand in that way? Did the silk of her dress rustle like that for Caius? Had Caius rioted at school when the pastry was bad? Had Caius fallen in love like that? Could Caius preside at a court session as he did? "Caius really was mortal, and it was right for him to die. But for me, little Vanya, Ivan Ilych, with all my thoughts and feelings, it is altogether different. It cannot be that I ought to die. That would be too terrible."

That was what he felt.

"If I had to die like Caius, I would have known it. Some inner voice would have told me. But there was nothing of that in me, and I and all my friends felt that our case was quite different from Caius's. And now here it is!" he said to himself. "It cannot be. It is impossible! But here it is. How can this be? How is one supposed to understand it?"

He could not understand it. He tried to drive away this false, incorrect, morbid thought and replace it with other proper and healthy thoughts. But the thought, and not only the thought but the reality itself, seemed to come and stand directly before him.

To replace that thought, he summoned one thought after another, hoping to find support in them. He tried to get back into the old current of thoughts that had once screened him from the thought of death. But strangely, everything that had formerly shut off, hidden, and destroyed his awareness of death no longer worked. Ivan now spent most of his time trying to restore that old current. He would say to himself: "I will take up my duties again. After all, I used to live by them." Then, pushing aside all doubts, he would go to the law courts, talk with his colleagues, and sit as he always had, carelessly scanning the crowd with a thoughtful look, both his thin arms resting on the arms of his oak chair. He would bend as usual toward a colleague, draw the papers nearer, whisper with him, then suddenly raise his eyes, sit upright, pronounce certain words, and open the proceedings.

But suddenly, in the middle of the proceedings, the pain in his side would begin its own gnawing work, with no respect for what stage the case had reached. Ivan would turn his attention to the pain and try to drive it away, but he could not. It would come and stand before him and look at him. He would become petrified. The light would die out of his eyes, and he would again ask himself whether It alone was true. The capital "It" matters: death has stopped being an idea and has become a presence in the room. His colleagues and subordinates would see, with surprise and distress, that the brilliant, subtle judge was becoming confused and making mistakes.

He would shake himself, try to pull himself together, somehow finish the sitting, and return home with the sorrowful knowledge that his judicial work could no longer hide from him what he wanted hidden. It could no longer deliver him from It. Worst of all, It drew his attention not so he could take some action, but only so he would look at It, look straight into its face, do nothing, and suffer beyond expression.

To save himself from this condition, Ivan looked for consolations, new screens. New screens were found and, for a while, seemed to protect him. But they immediately fell to pieces, or rather became transparent, as if It shone through them and nothing could veil It.

In these later days he would go into the drawing room he had arranged, the same drawing room where he had fallen, the room for whose sake, as it now seemed bitterly ridiculous, he had sacrificed his life. He knew his illness had begun with that knock. He would enter and see that something had scratched the polished table. He would look for the cause and find that the bronze ornament on an album had been bent. He would pick up the expensive album he had lovingly arranged and become vexed at his daughter and her friends for their disorder. The album was torn here and there, and some photographs had been turned upside down. He would carefully put it in order and bend the ornament back into place. Then it would occur to him to move all those things to another corner of the room, near the plants. He would call the footman, but his daughter or wife would come to help. They would disagree with him. His wife would contradict him. He would argue and become angry. But that was all right, because then he was not thinking about It. It was invisible.

But then, when he was moving something himself, his wife would say, "Let the servants do it. You will hurt yourself again." Suddenly It would flash through the screen and he would see it. It was only a flash, and he would hope it would disappear, but involuntarily his attention would go to his side. "It sits there as before, gnawing just the same!" And he could no longer forget It. He could distinctly see It looking at him from behind the flowers. "What is it all for?"

"It really is so! I lost my life over that curtain, as I might have lost it storming a fort. Is that possible? How terrible and how stupid. It cannot be true! It cannot, but it is."

He would go to his study, lie down, and again be alone with It: face to face with It. Nothing could be done with It except to look at it and shudder.

## Chapter VII: Gerasim and the Only Honest Comfort

### Before you read

Before reading: Gerasim's simplicity is not stupidity. He is the only person who tells the truth about death without cruelty, and that makes him the only person who can comfort Ivan.

### Jerry Edition (direct rewrite across the full novella; preserves source order and important quotes)

It is impossible to say exactly how it happened, because it happened step by step and went almost unnoticed. But by the third month of Ivan Ilych's illness, his wife, daughter, son, acquaintances, doctors, servants, and above all Ivan himself all knew that his whole interest for other people had become one question: would he soon vacate his place? Would he at last release the living from the discomfort caused by his presence, and would he himself be released from his sufferings?

He slept less and less. He was given opium and injections of morphine, but they did not relieve him. At first the dull depression that came in his half-sleeping state gave him a little relief, simply because it was something new. Later it became as distressing as the pain itself, or even more distressing.

Special foods were prepared for him according to the doctors' orders. But all those foods became more and more distasteful and disgusting to him.

Special arrangements also had to be made for his bodily waste. Every time, this was torment to him: torment from the uncleanness, the indecency, the smell, and from knowing that another person had to take part in it.

Yet through this most unpleasant matter, Ivan found comfort. Gerasim, the butler's young assistant, always came in to carry things out. Gerasim was a clean, fresh peasant lad, grown stout on town food, always cheerful and bright. At first, seeing him in his clean Russian peasant clothes doing that disgusting task embarrassed Ivan.

Once, after Ivan got up from the commode too weak to pull up his trousers, he dropped into a soft armchair and looked with horror at his bare, weakened thighs, the muscles standing out sharply on them.

Gerasim came in with a firm, light step. His heavy boots gave off a pleasant smell of tar and fresh winter air. He wore a clean coarse apron, and the sleeves of his printed shirt were tucked up over his strong bare young arms. Out of consideration for his sick master, he tried not to look at him, and he restrained the joy of life that beamed from his face. Then he went up to the commode.

"Gerasim!" Ivan said in a weak voice.

Gerasim started, plainly afraid he had made some mistake, and quickly turned his fresh, kind, simple young face toward Ivan. The first downy signs of a beard showed on it.

"Yes, sir?"

"That must be very unpleasant for you. You must forgive me. I am helpless."

"Oh, why, sir," Gerasim said, his eyes shining and his white teeth showing. "What's a little trouble? It is a case of illness with you, sir."

His strong, skillful hands did their usual task, and he stepped lightly out of the room. Five minutes later he returned just as lightly.

Ivan was still sitting in the same position in the armchair.

"Gerasim," he said, when Gerasim had replaced the freshly washed vessel. "Please come here and help me." Gerasim came up to him. "Lift me up. It is hard for me to get up, and I have sent Dmitri away."

Gerasim went to him, took his master in his strong arms, skillfully and gently, in the same way he moved. He lifted him, supported him with one hand, and with the other pulled up his trousers. He was about to set him down again, but Ivan asked to be led to the sofa. Without effort and without seeming to press him, Gerasim led him, almost lifting him, to the sofa and placed him on it.

"Thank you. How easily and well you do it all!"

Gerasim smiled again and turned to leave. But Ivan felt such comfort from his presence that he did not want to let him go.

"One more thing. Please move up that chair. No, the other one, under my feet. It is easier for me when my feet are raised."

Gerasim brought the chair, set it gently in place, and raised Ivan's legs onto it. It seemed to Ivan that he felt better while Gerasim was holding up his legs.

"It is better when my legs are higher," he said. "Put that cushion under them."

Gerasim did so. He lifted the legs again and placed them, and again Ivan felt better while Gerasim held them. When Gerasim set them down, Ivan imagined he felt worse.

"Gerasim," he said. "Are you busy now?"

"Not at all, sir," said Gerasim, who had learned from townspeople how to speak to gentlefolk.

"What do you still have to do?"

"What do I have to do? I have done everything except chopping the logs for tomorrow."

"Then hold my legs up a little higher, can you?"

"Of course I can. Why not?" Gerasim raised his master's legs higher, and Ivan thought that in that position he felt no pain at all.

"And what about the logs?"

"Don't trouble about that, sir. There is plenty of time."

Ivan told Gerasim to sit down and hold his legs, and he began talking to him. Strange to say, it seemed to Ivan that he felt better while Gerasim held his legs up.

After that, Ivan would sometimes call Gerasim and have him hold his legs on his shoulders, and he liked talking to him. Gerasim did it all easily, willingly, simply, and with a good nature that touched Ivan. Health, strength, and vitality in other people offended him, but Gerasim's strength and vitality did not humiliate him. They soothed him.

What tormented Ivan most was the deception, the lie that everyone accepted for some reason: that he was not dying, only ill, and that if he kept quiet and followed treatment something very good would result. But Ivan knew that whatever they did, nothing would come except more agonizing suffering and death. This lie tortured him: they did not want to admit what they all knew and what he knew, and they wanted, and forced, him to participate in the lie.

These lies were acted out over him on the eve of his death. They degraded the awful, solemn act of dying to the level of their visits, their curtains, their sturgeon for dinner. That was a terrible agony for Ivan. Many times, strangely enough, when they were performing their little routines over him, he had been within a hair's breadth of crying out: "Stop lying! You know and I know that I am dying. Then at least stop lying about it!" But he had never had the strength to do it.

He saw that the awful, terrible act of his dying had been reduced by everyone around him to a casual, unpleasant, almost indecorous incident, as if someone had entered a drawing room carrying a bad smell. And this reduction was done by the very decorum he had served all his life. That is the reversal: the polite social world he worshiped now refuses to recognize the one thing that is actually happening to him.

He saw that no one felt for him, because no one even wanted to grasp his position. Only Gerasim recognized it and pitied him. So Ivan felt at ease only with Gerasim. He was comforted when Gerasim supported his legs, sometimes all night, and refused to go to bed, saying, "Don't you worry, Ivan Ilych. I will get sleep enough later on." Or when Gerasim suddenly became familiar and exclaimed, "If you were not sick it would be another matter, but as it is, why should I grudge a little trouble?"

Gerasim alone did not lie. Everything showed that he alone understood the facts and did not think it necessary to disguise them. He simply felt sorry for his thin, weakened master. Once, when Ivan tried to send him away, Gerasim even said directly, "We shall all of us die, so why should I grudge a little trouble?" He meant that the work was not burdensome because he was doing it for a dying man, and he hoped someone would do the same for him when his own time came.

Apart from this lying, or because of it, what tormented Ivan most was that no one pitied him as he wanted to be pitied. At certain moments after prolonged suffering, what he wanted most, though he would have been ashamed to confess it, was for someone to pity him as a sick child is pitied. He longed to be petted and comforted. He knew he was an important official, that his beard was turning gray, and therefore that such a wish was impossible. But still he longed for it.

In Gerasim's attitude there was something like what Ivan wanted, and so it comforted him. Ivan wanted to weep, wanted to be petted and cried over. Then his colleague Shebek would come, and instead of weeping and being petted, Ivan would assume a serious, severe, profound expression and, from force of habit, give his opinion on a decision of the Court of Cassation and stubbornly insist on it. This falseness around him, and inside him, poisoned his last days more than anything else.

## Chapter VIII: Family Life Continues Around Him

It was morning. Ivan knew it was morning because Gerasim had gone, and Peter the footman had come in, put out the candles, drawn back one curtain, and begun quietly tidying the room. Whether it was morning or evening, Friday or Sunday, made no difference. It was all the same: the gnawing, unrelieved, agonizing pain that never stopped for an instant; the awareness that life was steadily fading but not yet gone; the approach of that dreaded and hateful Death, which was the only reality; and always the same falsehood around him. What were days, weeks, or hours in such a condition?

"Will you have some tea, sir?"

"He wants things to be regular, and gentlefolk are supposed to drink tea in the morning," Ivan thought, and said only, "No."

"Wouldn't you like to move onto the sofa, sir?"

"He wants to tidy the room, and I am in the way. I am uncleanness and disorder," Ivan thought, and said only:

"Oh, tea! All right, put it down. Only help me wash and put on a clean shirt."

He came in fresh, hearty, plump, and cheerful, with an expression that seemed to say: "There now, you are panicking about something, but we will arrange it all for you directly!" The doctor rubbed his hands vigorously and reassuringly.

"Brr! How cold it is! Such a sharp frost. Just let me warm myself!" he said, as if everything were only a matter of waiting until he was warm, and then he would put it right.

"Well now, how are you?"

He submitted just as he used to submit to lawyers' speeches, though he knew very well that they were all lying and knew why they were lying.

The doctor was still kneeling on the sofa and sounding him when Praskovya Fedorovna's silk dress rustled at the door. Ivan heard her scolding Peter for not telling her the doctor had arrived.

She came in, kissed her husband, and immediately began proving that she had been up for a long time already and had failed to be there when the doctor arrived only because of a misunderstanding.

Ivan looked at her and scanned her all over. He noticed the whiteness, plumpness, and cleanness of her hands and neck, the gloss of her hair, and the sparkle of her lively eyes. He hated her with his whole soul. The thrill of hatred he felt at her touch made him suffer.

Her attitude toward him and his disease was still the same. Just as the doctor had adopted a relation to his patient that he could not abandon, she had adopted one too: Ivan was not doing something he ought to do; he himself was to blame; and she reproached him lovingly for it. Now she could not change that attitude.

She described how he made Gerasim hold his legs.

The doctor smiled with contemptuous friendliness, as if to say: "What can one do? Sick people do have foolish fancies of that kind, but we must forgive them."

When the examination was over, the doctor looked at his watch. Then Praskovya Fedorovna announced to Ivan that it was of course as he pleased, but she had sent that day for a celebrated specialist, who would examine him and consult with Michael Danilovich, their regular doctor.

"Please do not raise any objections. I am doing this for my own sake," she said ironically, making him feel that of course she was doing it all for him, and was only saying "for my own sake" so that he would have no right to refuse. He stayed silent, frowning. He felt surrounded and entangled in such a net of falseness that it was hard to untangle anything.

Everything she did for him was really done for herself. Then she told him she was doing for herself what she was in fact doing for herself, but in such a tone that he was supposed to understand the opposite.

At half-past eleven, the celebrated specialist arrived. Again came the sounding, and again the important conversations in Ivan's presence and in another room about kidney and appendix. Again came the questions and answers, all with such an air of importance that the real question confronting Ivan, the question of life and death, was displaced by the question of kidney and appendix. These organs, apparently, were not behaving as they ought, and now Michael Danilovich and the specialist would attack them and force them to mend their ways.

The celebrated specialist left with a serious but not hopeless look. Ivan, with eyes shining from fear and hope, timidly asked whether there was a chance of recovery. Ivan watched him leave with such a pathetic look of hope that Praskovya Fedorovna, seeing it, even wept as she left the room to pay the doctor's fee.

The gleam of hope lit by the doctor's encouragement did not last long. The same room was there, the same pictures, curtains, wallpaper, medicine bottles, and the same aching, suffering body. Ivan began to moan. They gave him an injection under the skin, and he sank into oblivion.

Ivan himself had insisted that they take it. Now he had forgotten, and her dressed-up appearance offended him. Still, he hid his irritation when he remembered that he himself had insisted on securing a box and going because it would be an instructive and aesthetic pleasure for the children.

Praskovya Fedorovna came in self-satisfied, though with a somewhat guilty air. She sat down and asked how he was, but only for the sake of asking, as he could see, not in order to learn anything. She already knew there was nothing to learn. Then she moved on to what she really wanted to say: she would not have gone for anything, but the box had been taken, Helen and their daughter were going, Petrishchev was going too, the examining magistrate who was their daughter's fiance, and it was out of the question to let them go alone. Of course she would have much preferred to sit with Ivan for a while. And he must be sure to follow the doctor's orders while she was away.

"Oh, and Fedor Petrovich," she added, meaning the fiance, "would like to come in. May he? And Lisa?"

"All right."

Their daughter came in dressed for the evening, her fresh young flesh exposed. That same flesh, which in Ivan's own case caused so much suffering, was displayed in her as strength, health, love, and impatience. She was evidently in love, and illness, suffering, and death annoyed her because they interfered with her happiness.

Fedor Petrovich came in too, in evening dress. His hair was curled a la Capoul, a fashionable theatrical style named after a performer; this is not just a haircut note, but a signal that he is polished, current, and stage-ready. A tight stiff collar circled his long sinewy neck, an enormous white shirtfront spread across him, and narrow black trousers stretched tightly over his strong thighs. He had one white glove pulled on and held his opera hat in his hand.

After him the schoolboy slipped in unnoticed, in a new uniform, poor little fellow, wearing gloves. Terribly dark shadows showed under his eyes, and Ivan knew very well what they meant.

His son had always seemed pathetic to him. Now the boy's frightened look of pity was dreadful to see. It seemed to Ivan that Vasya, besides Gerasim, was the only one who understood and pitied him.

They all sat down and again asked how he was. Silence followed. Lisa asked her mother about the opera glasses, and mother and daughter argued about who had taken them and where they had been put. This caused some unpleasantness.

Fedor Petrovich asked Ivan whether he had ever seen Sarah Bernhardt. At first Ivan did not catch the question, then answered, "No. Have you seen her before?"

"Yes, in Adrienne Lecouvreur."

Praskovya Fedorovna mentioned some roles in which Sarah Bernhardt was especially good. Her daughter disagreed. A conversation sprang up about the elegance and realism of Bernhardt's acting, the sort of conversation that is always repeated and always the same. The play talk matters because it mirrors the room: everyone is discussing performance while performing normal family life around a dying man.

In the middle of the conversation, Fedor Petrovich glanced at Ivan and fell silent. The others looked at him too and grew silent. Ivan was staring straight ahead with glittering eyes, plainly indignant with them. This had to be repaired, but it could not be repaired. The silence had to be broken, but for a while no one dared break it. They all became afraid that the conventional deception would suddenly become obvious and the truth would become plain to everyone. Lisa was the first to gather courage and break the silence, but by trying to hide what everyone felt, she betrayed it.

"Well, if we are going, it is time to start," she said, looking at her watch, a present from her father, and giving Fedor Petrovich a faint, significant smile about something known only to them. She got up with a rustle of her dress.

They all rose, said good night, and went away.

When they had gone, it seemed to Ivan that he felt better. The falseness had gone away with them. But the pain remained, the same pain and the same fear that made everything monotonously alike. Nothing became harder, and nothing became easier. Everything was worse.

Again minute followed minute and hour followed hour. Everything remained the same. There was no pause. The inevitable end of it all became more and more terrible.

"Yes, send Gerasim here," he answered when Peter asked a question.

## Chapter IX: What If My Whole Life Was Wrong?

### Before you read

Before reading: this is the central self-judgment chapter. Ivan does not merely fear pain; he begins to suspect that the entire shape of his respectable life was wrong.

His wife returned late at night. She came in on tiptoe, but he heard her, opened his eyes, and hurried to close them again. She wanted to send Gerasim away and sit with him herself, but he opened his eyes and said, "No, go away."

"Are you in great pain?"

"Always the same."

"Take some opium."

He agreed and took some. She went away.

Until about three in the morning he was in a state of stupefied misery. It seemed to him that he and his pain were being pushed into a narrow, deep black sack. They were pushed farther and farther in, but could not be pushed to the bottom. That alone was terrible enough, and it came with suffering. He was frightened and yet wanted to fall through the sack. He struggled and yet also cooperated. Suddenly he broke through, fell, and regained consciousness. Gerasim was sitting at the foot of the bed, quietly and patiently dozing, while Ivan lay with his thin, stockinged legs resting on Gerasim's shoulders. The same shaded candle was there, and the same unceasing pain.

"Go away, Gerasim," he whispered.

"It is all right, sir. I will stay awhile."

"No. Go away."

He removed his legs from Gerasim's shoulders, turned sideways onto his arm, and felt sorry for himself. He waited only until Gerasim had gone into the next room. Then he stopped restraining himself and wept like a child. He wept because of his helplessness, his terrible loneliness, the cruelty of human beings, the cruelty of God, and the absence of God.

"Why have You done all this? Why have You brought me here? Why, why do You torment me so terribly?"

He did not expect an answer, and yet he wept because there was no answer and could be none. The pain grew sharper again, but he did not move and did not call. He said to himself, "Go on! Strike me! But what is it for? What have I done to You? What is it for?"

Then he became quiet. He not only stopped weeping, but even held his breath and became all attention. It was as if he were listening, not to an audible voice, but to the voice of his soul, to the current of thoughts rising within him.

"What is it you want?" was the first clear thought he could put into words.

"What do you want? What do you want?" he repeated to himself.

"What do I want? To live and not suffer," he answered.

Again he listened with such concentrated attention that even the pain did not distract him.

"To live? How?" asked the inner voice.

"Why, to live as I used to, well and pleasantly."

"As you lived before, well and pleasantly?" the voice repeated.

In imagination he began recalling the best moments of his pleasant life. But strangely, none of those best moments now seemed at all what they had seemed then, except the first memories of childhood. There, in childhood, there had been something really pleasant, something it would be possible to live with if it could return. But the child who had experienced that happiness no longer existed. It was like a memory of someone else.

As soon as he reached the period that had produced the present Ivan Ilych, all the things that had seemed joyful began to melt before his sight and turn into something trivial and often nasty.

The farther he moved from childhood and the nearer he came to the present, the more worthless and doubtful the joys became. It began at the School of Law. A little that was really good was still there: light-heartedness, friendship, hope. But in the upper classes there were already fewer such good moments. Then, in his first years of official work in the governor's service, pleasant moments appeared again, memories of love for a woman. After that everything became confused and there was still less good. Later there was less again, and the farther he went, the less there was.

His marriage had been a mere accident, followed by disenchantment: his wife's bad breath, sensuality, hypocrisy. Then came the deadly official life and the money worries, one year of it, then two, ten, twenty, always the same. The longer it lasted, the more deadening it became. "It is as if I had been going downhill while imagining I was going up. And that is really what it was. I was rising in public opinion, but to the same extent life was ebbing away from me. And now it is all done, and there is only death."

"Then what does it mean? Why? It cannot be that life is so senseless and horrible. But if it really has been so horrible and senseless, why must I die, and die in agony? There is something wrong!"

"Maybe I did not live as I ought to have done," suddenly occurred to him. "But how could that be, when I did everything properly?" he answered, and immediately dismissed this thought, the only solution to all the riddles of life and death, as something completely impossible.

"Then what do you want now? To live? Live how? Live as you lived in the law courts when the usher proclaimed, 'The judge is coming!' The judge is coming, the judge!" he repeated to himself. "Here he is, the judge. But I am not guilty!" he exclaimed angrily. "What is it for?" He stopped crying, turned his face to the wall, and kept thinking about the same question: why, and for what purpose, is all this horror? But no matter how much he thought, he found no answer. Whenever the thought came to him, as it often did, that all of this resulted from his not having lived as he ought, he at once remembered the correctness of his whole life and dismissed so strange an idea.

## Chapter X: The Scream Inside the Drawing Room

### Before you read

Before reading: the black sack image is Ivan's felt experience of dying: being pushed toward what he cannot escape while still unable to pass through.

Another two weeks passed. Ivan no longer left his sofa. He would not lie in bed, but lay on the sofa, facing the wall almost all the time. He suffered the same unceasing agonies, and in his loneliness kept thinking about the same insoluble question: "What is this? Can it be Death?" The inner voice answered, "Yes, it is Death."

"Why these sufferings?" The voice answered, "For no reason. They simply are." Beyond that, and besides that, there was nothing.

From the beginning of his illness, ever since his first visit to the doctor, Ivan's life had been divided between two opposite moods that alternated. One was despair and the expectation of this incomprehensible and terrible death. The other was hope and an intensely interested observation of how his organs were functioning. At one moment all he saw was a kidney or intestine temporarily failing in its duty; at the next he saw only that incomprehensible and dreadful death from which there was no escape.

These two states had alternated from the beginning of the illness. But the farther the illness went, the more doubtful and fantastic the kidney became, and the more real the approaching death became.

He only had to remember what he had been three months before and compare it with what he was now; he only had to remember how steadily he had been going downhill, and every possibility of hope was shattered.

In these later days, during the loneliness in which he lay facing the back of the sofa, loneliness in a crowded town, surrounded by acquaintances and relatives, yet more complete than loneliness at the bottom of the sea or under the earth, Ivan lived only in memories of the past. Pictures of his past rose before him one after another. They always began with what was nearest in time, then moved backward to what was most remote, to childhood, and rested there.

If he thought of the stewed prunes offered to him that day, his mind went back to the raw, shriveled French plums of his childhood, their peculiar flavor and the saliva that came when he sucked their stones. Along with that taste came a whole series of memories: his nurse, his brother, their toys. "No, I must not think of that. It is too painful," Ivan said to himself, and brought himself back to the present: the button on the back of the sofa, the creases in its morocco leather. "Morocco is expensive, but it does not wear well. There had been a quarrel about it. It was a different quarrel and a different morocco that time when we tore father's portfolio and were punished, and mama brought us some tarts..." Again his thoughts dwelt on childhood. Again it was painful, and he tried to banish them and fix his mind on something else.

Then, together with that chain of memories, another series passed through his mind: how his illness had progressed and grown worse. There too, the farther back he looked, the more life there had been. There had been more good in life and more life itself. The two series merged. "Just as the pain has gone on getting worse and worse, so my life grew worse and worse," he thought. "There is one bright spot back there, at the beginning of life, and afterward everything becomes blacker and blacker and moves more and more quickly, in inverse ratio to the square of the distance from death." This mathematical phrase means the fall accelerates: the closer he gets to death, the faster life seems to rush downward. The image of a stone falling faster and faster entered his mind. Life, a series of increasing sufferings, flies faster and faster toward its end, the most terrible suffering. "I am flying..." He shuddered, shifted himself, and tried to resist. But he already knew resistance was impossible. Again, with eyes tired of looking but unable to stop seeing what was before them, he stared at the back of the sofa and waited, awaiting that dreadful fall, shock, and destruction.

"Resistance is impossible!" he said to himself. "If only I could understand what it is all for! But that too is impossible. An explanation would be possible if it could be said that I have not lived as I ought. But it is impossible to say that." He remembered all the legality, correctness, and propriety of his life. "That at any rate certainly cannot be admitted," he thought, and his lips smiled ironically, as if someone could see the smile and be deceived by it. "There is no explanation! Agony, death. What for?"

## Chapter XI: The False Life Comes Into View

Another two weeks went by in this way. During that fortnight, an event occurred that Ivan and his wife had wanted: Petrishchev formally proposed to their daughter. It happened in the evening. The next day Praskovya Fedorovna came into her husband's room, thinking about the best way to tell him. But that very night there had been a new change for the worse in his condition. She found him still lying on the sofa, but in a different position. He lay on his back, groaning and staring fixedly straight ahead.

She began reminding him about his medicines, but he turned his eyes toward her with such a look that she did not finish what she was saying. That look expressed such fierce hostility, especially toward her.

"For Christ's sake, let me die in peace!" he said.

She would have gone away, but just then their daughter came in and came up to say good morning. He looked at her as he had looked at his wife, and in answer to her question about his health said dryly that he would soon free them all from himself. They were both silent. After sitting with him for a while, they went away.

"Is it our fault?" Lisa said to her mother. "It is as if we were to blame! I am sorry for papa, but why should we be tortured?"

The doctor came at his usual time. Ivan answered "Yes" and "No," never taking his angry eyes off him, and at last said, "You know you can do nothing for me, so leave me alone."

"We can ease your sufferings."

"You cannot even do that. Let me be."

The doctor went into the drawing room and told Praskovya Fedorovna that the case was very serious and that the only resource left was opium to lessen her husband's sufferings, which must be terrible.

It was true, as the doctor said, that Ivan's physical sufferings were terrible. But worse than the physical sufferings were his mental sufferings, and they were his chief torture.

His mental suffering came from this: that night, as he looked at Gerasim's sleepy, good-natured face with its prominent cheekbones, the question had suddenly occurred to him: "What if my whole life has been wrong?"

It occurred to him that what had previously seemed completely impossible, that he had not lived his life as he should have, might after all be true. It occurred to him that his barely perceptible attempts to resist what the highest-ranking people considered good, those scarcely noticeable impulses he had immediately suppressed, might have been the real thing, and all the rest might have been false. His professional duties, the whole arrangement of his life and family, all his social and official interests, might all have been false. He tried to defend those things to himself and suddenly felt how weak his defense was. There was nothing to defend.

"But if that is so," he said to himself, "and I am leaving life knowing that I have lost everything given to me and that it is impossible to set it right, what then?"

He lay on his back and began reviewing his life in an entirely new way. In the morning, when he saw first the footman, then his wife, then his daughter, then the doctor, every word and movement of theirs confirmed the awful truth revealed to him during the night. In them he saw himself, saw everything he had lived for, and saw clearly that it was not real at all. It was a terrible and enormous deception that had hidden both life and death. This awareness multiplied his physical suffering tenfold. He groaned, tossed about, and pulled at his clothing because it choked and suffocated him. And because of this, he hated them.

He was given a large dose of opium and became unconscious, but at noon his sufferings began again. He drove everyone away and tossed from side to side.

His wife came to him and said:

"Jean, my dear, do this for me. It cannot do any harm, and it often helps. Healthy people often do it."

He opened his eyes wide.

"What? Take communion? Why? It is unnecessary! However..."

She began to cry.

"Yes, do, my dear. I will send for our priest. He is such a nice man."

"All right. Very well," he muttered.

When the priest came and heard his confession, Ivan softened. He seemed to feel relief from his doubts and therefore from his sufferings, and for a moment a ray of hope came to him. He again began thinking of the appendix and the possibility of correcting it. He received the sacrament with tears in his eyes.

When they laid him down afterward, he felt a moment's ease, and hope that he might live awoke in him again. He began thinking of the operation that had been suggested. "To live! I want to live!" he said to himself.

His wife came in to congratulate him after communion, and while saying the usual conventional words, she added:

"You feel better, don't you?"

Without looking at her, he said, "Yes."

Her dress, her figure, the expression on her face, the tone of her voice, all revealed the same thing. "This is wrong. It is not as it should be. Everything you have lived for and still live for is falsehood and deception, hiding life and death from you." As soon as he admitted that thought, his hatred and agonizing physical suffering sprang up again, and with the suffering came awareness of the unavoidable approaching end. Added to this was a new sensation of grinding, shooting pain and suffocation.

The expression on his face when he said "Yes" was dreadful. After saying it, he looked straight into her eyes, turned over onto his face with extraordinary speed for someone so weak, and shouted:

"Go away! Go away and leave me alone!"

## Chapter XII: Pity, Light, and the End of Death

### Before you read

From that moment the screaming began. It continued for three days, and it was so terrible that no one could hear it through two closed doors without horror. At the moment Ivan answered his wife, she realized that he was lost, that there was no return, that the end had come, the very end, and that his doubts were still unresolved and remained doubts.

"Oh! Oh! Oh!" he cried in different tones. He had begun by screaming, "I won't!" and then continued screaming on the sound "O."

For three whole days, during which time did not exist for him, he struggled in the black sack into which an invisible, irresistible force was pushing him. He struggled as a man condemned to death struggles in the hands of the executioner, knowing he cannot save himself. Every moment he felt that despite all his efforts he was drawing nearer and nearer to what terrified him. He felt that his agony came from being pushed into that black hole, and even more from not being able to get all the way into it. What prevented him from getting in was his conviction that his life had been good. That very justification of his life held him fast and kept him from moving forward, and it caused him the most torment of all.

Suddenly some force struck him in the chest and side, making it still harder to breathe. He fell through the hole, and there at the bottom was light. What happened to him was like the sensation one sometimes has in a railway carriage, when one thinks one is going backward while actually going forward and suddenly becomes aware of the real direction.

"Yes, it was not the right thing," he said to himself, "but that does not matter. It can be done." What does he mean? Not that the plot can be repaired, but that the false life can be answered even now by pity instead of self-defense. "But what is the right thing?" he asked himself, and suddenly grew quiet.

This happened at the end of the third day, two hours before his death. Just then his schoolboy son had crept quietly in and gone up to the bedside. The dying man was still screaming desperately and waving his arms. His hand fell on the boy's head. The boy caught it, pressed it to his lips, and began to cry.

At that very moment Ivan fell through and caught sight of the light. It was revealed to him that although his life had not been what it should have been, this could still be corrected. He asked himself, "What is the right thing?" and became still, listening. Then he felt someone kissing his hand. He opened his eyes, looked at his son, and felt sorry for him. His wife came up to him, and he glanced at her. She was gazing at him open-mouthed, with undried tears on her nose and cheek and a despairing look on her face. He felt sorry for her too.

"Yes, I am making them wretched," he thought. "They are sorry, but it will be better for them when I die." He wanted to say this but did not have the strength to utter it. "Besides, why speak? I must act," he thought. With a look at his wife, he indicated his son and said, "Take him away...sorry for him...sorry for you too..." He tried to add, "Forgive me," but said "Forego," and waved his hand, knowing that He whose understanding mattered would understand.

Suddenly it became clear to him that what had been oppressing him and would not leave him was dropping away all at once, from two sides, from ten sides, from every side. He was sorry for them. He had to act so as not to hurt them: release them and free himself from these sufferings. "How good and how simple!" he thought. "And the pain?" he asked himself. "What has become of it? Where are you, pain?"

He turned his attention to it.

"Yes, here it is. Well, what of it? Let the pain be."

"And death? Where is it?"

He looked for his old familiar fear of death and did not find it. "Where is it? What death?" There was no fear because there was no death.

In place of death there was light.

"So that is what it is!" he suddenly exclaimed aloud. "What joy!"

For him all this happened in a single instant, and the meaning of that instant did not change. For those present, his agony continued another two hours. Something rattled in his throat, his wasted body twitched, and then the gasping and rattling became less and less frequent.

"It is finished!" someone near him said.

He heard these words and repeated them in his soul.

"Death is finished," he said to himself. "It is no more!"

He drew in a breath, stopped in the middle of a sigh, stretched out, and died.
